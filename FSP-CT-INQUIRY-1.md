# Inquiry Begins Before Science Has a Name

Human beings were inquirers before there were laboratories, scientific journals, universities, or formal theories of method.

We notice patterns. We compare cases. We remember what happened before. We anticipate what might happen next. We use tools. We coordinate with others. We make distinctions, build explanations, test expectations against resistance, and revise what we thought we knew.

Over time, some of these practices become more explicit, more disciplined, more public, more instrumented, and more institutionally organized.

The history of inquiry is therefore not simply the history of correct theories replacing incorrect ones.

It is also the history of changing ways to observe, represent, compare, measure, explain, argue, test, model, authorize, revise, and share knowledge.

This course asks us to follow those changes.

It also asks us to notice that inquiry is always conducted by situated human beings using limited instruments, inherited concepts, social institutions, learned practices, and partial evidence.

That means critical thinking in the history of inquiry requires more than checking whether an argument is valid.

We will also ask:

- What was actually observed?
- What had to be represented before it could be compared?
- Which instruments made the phenomenon visible?
- What did a model explain, and what did it leave open?
- Which alternatives remained live?
- What did the evidence actually license?
- Which background assumptions shaped what counted as a problem?
- Who had standing to certify a result?
- What institutional practices made a claim durable?
- What anomaly or consequence forced revision?
- What does not yet follow?
- What would REOPEN the inquiry?

Those questions form the governed-critical-thinking layer of this course.

---

# 1. Observation Is Already Situated

Scientific inquiry often begins with observation, but observation is never simply “the world entering the mind unchanged.”

What can be observed depends partly on bodily capacities, instruments, learned distinctions, concepts, procedures, recording practices, and historical expectations.

A telescope, microscope, clock, interferometer, spectrograph, statistical model, simulation, and survey each make different features available for inquiry.

Even before instrumentation, attention is selective.

So the first recurring rule of this course is:

> **Map ≠ World.**

A map can be a diagram, measurement, data table, coordinate system, model, theory, reconstruction, simulation, or verbal description.

Maps are indispensable.

But they are not identical with what they represent.

This gives us an important inquiry habit:

> **Before asking whether a conclusion is correct, ask what representation made the conclusion possible.**

That question will matter in Galileo, Foucault, Neptune and Vulcan, Newton and Einstein, cosmology, and contemporary data-intensive science.

---

# 2. Instruments Do Not Merely Add More Facts

Instruments can extend what humans can detect, measure, compare, record, and reproduce.

But using an instrument also requires interpretation.

A reading has to be calibrated.

Noise has to be distinguished from signal.

A measurement has to be connected to a quantity.

A procedure has to be repeatable enough to matter.

A result has to be placed inside a conceptual framework.

So:

> **Instrument reading ≠ uninterpreted fact.**

This does not make measurement arbitrary.

It means that evidence comes through practices that must themselves be inspected.

The governing questions include:

**What did the instrument directly register?**

**What transformations produced the reported quantity?**

**Which assumptions connect the reading to the claim?**

**What uncertainty remains?**

This is one of the places where ordinary critical thinking expands into governed inquiry.

---

# 3. Inquiry Has Assembly Histories Too

The questions scientists ask, the concepts they inherit, the instruments they trust, the distinctions they draw, and the standards they use all have histories.

Scientific communities do not begin from zero.

They inherit languages, mathematical techniques, experimental traditions, institutional roles, textbooks, observatories, laboratories, accepted constants, classification systems, and expectations about what counts as an explanation.

These inherited structures make inquiry possible.

They can also make alternatives harder to see.

We will use **assembly history** to name the reciprocal history through which inquiry practices, concepts, instruments, institutions, and investigators become capable of doing the work they now do.

But we must preserve a crucial distinction:

> **Origin ≠ validity.**

Showing how a scientific idea arose does not prove it false.

Likewise:

> **Historical formation ≠ present justification.**

A theory can have a complicated history and still be well supported.

A method can be historically prestigious and still deserve criticism.

The history of inquiry matters because it helps us understand how a field came to see what it sees—not because historical origin settles truth.

---

# 4. Reflective Inquiry Makes Method Inspectable

Much successful inquiry happens before investigators explicitly theorize their own methods.

Scientists often learn by doing: observing, measuring, calculating, experimenting, comparing, correcting, and communicating.

Reflection becomes explicit when investigators begin asking questions such as:

**What exactly did we measure?**

**What assumptions entered the inference?**

**Why should this result count as evidence?**

**What else could explain the observation?**

**How much confidence is warranted?**

**What would count against the model?**

**What would make us revise the framework itself?**

This reflective turn is where familiar critical-thinking skills remain central.

We will still use deduction, induction, abduction, analogy, causal reasoning, source evaluation, probability, uncertainty, counterexamples, and argument reconstruction.

FSP does not replace those tools.

It extends attention to the transitions surrounding them.

---

# 5. From Critical Thinking to Governed Inquiry

A useful way to picture scientific and historical inquiry is:

```text
encounter / observation
↔ selective articulation / representation
↔ question
↔ hypothesis / model
↔ evidence
↔ inferential license
↔ alternatives
↔ bounded epistemic judgment
↔ institutional validation
↔ anomaly / resistance
↔ revision / REOPEN
```

This is not a universal scientific method.

Real inquiry is messier.

Observation can change a model.

A model can change what investigators look for.

New instruments can create new questions.

An anomaly can be ignored, absorbed, reinterpreted, or become revolutionary.

Institutional acceptance can lag behind evidence.

A result can be reliable while its deeper interpretation remains unsettled.

The value of the framework is diagnostic:

> **What kind of inquiry problem are we facing now?**

---

# 6. Six Recurring Questions for the History of Inquiry

The full FSP architecture contains twelve canonical Tools, but this Pressbook does not need to foreground all twelve equally.

For History of Inquiry, six recurring questions do most of the introductory work.

## 1. What is the map?

What was observed, represented, measured, modeled, or reconstructed?

What has been added by the representation?

## 2. What licenses the inference?

What reasons connect the evidence to the conclusion?

What follows, and what does not?

## 3. What alternatives remain live?

Could the same evidence support more than one explanation?

What auxiliary assumptions are doing work?

## 4. What shaped the inquiry?

Which inherited concepts, instruments, institutions, mathematical techniques, or background commitments influenced what counted as evidence or explanation?

## 5. What is the claim ceiling?

What is the strongest conclusion the evidence earns?

What does not yet follow?

## 6. What would REOPEN this?

What new evidence, failed prediction, anomaly, conceptual change, or better explanation would force revision?

These six questions are enough to begin.

The later chapters will deepen them through real historical cases.

---

# 7. Models Are Powerful Without Being the World

One of the most important distinctions in this course is:

> **Model success ≠ ontology.**

A model may predict accurately.

It may organize observations elegantly.

It may unify previously separate phenomena.

It may guide successful intervention.

None of those facts by itself establishes that the model's entities, structure, or interpretation are the final description of reality.

This matters repeatedly in the history of science.

Newtonian gravitation worked extraordinarily well before Einstein.

The success of epicycles did not settle planetary ontology.

A statistical model can fit data without revealing the true causal structure.

A cosmological model can be strongly supported while deeper interpretation remains open.

So the question is not whether models are “mere constructions.”

The question is:

> **What has this model earned?**

That is a claim-ceiling question.

---

# 8. Evidence Does Not Choose a Theory by Itself

Historical inquiry repeatedly shows that evidence is interpreted within networks of assumptions.

A failed prediction may reflect:

- the central theory;
- an auxiliary hypothesis;
- an instrument;
- a background assumption;
- a calculation;
- an unmodeled effect;
- an incorrect initial condition.

This is one reason the Neptune/Vulcan comparison is so instructive.

Unexpected planetary behavior did not immediately tell investigators which part of the theoretical system should be revised.

In one case, modifying the assumed contents of the solar system succeeded.

In the other, a deeper theoretical change eventually became necessary.

The critical-thinking lesson is not:

> “One anomaly falsifies a theory.”

It is:

> **An anomaly creates an inquiry problem whose location must be diagnosed.**

This is where Duhem–Quine becomes useful.

Evidence constrains inquiry.

It does not always determine one uniquely forced theoretical response.

---

# 9. Meaning Can Change with Frameworks

Kuhn adds another difficulty.

Scientific change can involve more than replacing one proposition with another.

Terms, exemplars, standards, classifications, and problem structures can shift.

What counts as an important question may change.

What counts as a satisfactory explanation may change.

Even apparently shared terms can acquire different roles within different theoretical structures.

This means that historical comparison requires care.

We cannot always assume:

> same word = same concept = same inferential role.

So the course will repeatedly distinguish:

> **Shared vocabulary ≠ shared conceptual structure.**

This is one reason historical reconstruction must remain separate from present-day FSP analysis.

We can use our current framework to inspect a historical episode without pretending the historical actors themselves possessed our categories.

---

# 10. Thought Experiments as Controlled Revision Spaces

Not every important inquiry intervention is an observation or laboratory experiment.

Thought experiments can reorganize a problem by controlling features of an imagined case.

Galileo's falling-body reasoning, Einstein's trains and elevators, and many other examples use imagined situations to expose tensions among assumptions, concepts, and consequences.

A thought experiment does not automatically establish a historical fact.

Nor does imagining a coherent scenario automatically prove that the world works that way.

So:

> **Probe result ≠ historical fact.**

and:

> **Possibility ≠ actuality.**

Thought experiments are powerful because they can isolate dependencies, pressure-test concepts, and reveal what follows from commitments.

They are controlled revision spaces.

---

# 11. Historical Episodes Are Not Tool Labels

The FSP framework is a modern reconstruction.

It should not be projected backward as though historical investigators were secretly following an FSP method.

Galileo did not use an FSP Tool Rack.

Foucault did not run a formal claim-ceiling audit.

Le Verrier did not label Neptune a transition state.

Einstein did not describe relativity as an FSP REOPEN event.

Instead, the course uses historical episodes to ask:

```text
historical problem
→ actual practices and evidence
→ reconstructed inquiry pressure
→ transferable critical-thinking function
→ bounded present-day lesson
```

This protects both history and method.

We do not turn history into a set of modern examples.

And we do not leave historical inquiry trapped in the past.

# 12. The Core Case Arc

Several cases will carry the course because each exposes a different kind of inquiry transition.

## Galileo

Galileo shows how observation, mathematics, instrumentation, argument, and conceptual restructuring can interact.

The key question is not simply whether Galileo was right.

It is how new evidence and new forms of representation altered what could be asked and defended.

## Foucault's Measurement of Light

Foucault's work makes measurement, apparatus, precision, and theory discrimination visible.

It shows how an experiment can do more than produce a number: it can help distinguish between rival expectations.

## Neptune and Vulcan

The paired case makes theory testing under auxiliary assumptions visible.

The same general strategy—locate an unseen cause—worked for Neptune and failed for Vulcan.

This prevents us from treating one successful research move as a universal rule.

## Newton → Einstein

This case makes domain, approximation, conceptual change, and model succession visible.

Einstein did not make Newton useless.

The history instead teaches us to ask where a model works, why it works there, and what changes when the domain expands.

## Duhem–Quine

This gives us a formal way to understand why evidence often bears on systems of assumptions rather than isolated statements.

## Kuhn and Meaning Change

This makes conceptual and disciplinary restructuring visible.

It also warns us that later scientists may not simply be answering exactly the same questions with better data.

## Thought Experiments

These make controlled conceptual revision visible.

They help us test dependencies before or even without direct experiment.

## DESI and Contemporary Cosmology

DESI brings the course into a live frontier.

Here students can see inquiry before history has settled it.

Data, models, statistical significance, competing interpretations, institutional collaboration, and claim ceilings all remain visible at once.

This is exactly where disciplined REOPEN matters.

---

# 13. Institutions Are Part of Scientific Inquiry

Science is not only an individual relationship between a thinker and evidence.

It is also a social and institutional achievement.

Observatories, laboratories, universities, journals, review practices, collaborations, funding systems, databases, standards bodies, professional communities, and instruments all shape what can be investigated and how results become durable.

These institutions can improve inquiry.

They can preserve expertise, distribute labor, enable replication, maintain instruments, coordinate large collaborations, and create public records.

They can also amplify conformity, hide assumptions, concentrate authority, delay criticism, or privilege some forms of evidence over others.

So we will ask:

**Who can make a claim visible?**

**Who can challenge it?**

**What counts as admissible evidence?**

**Who controls instruments or data?**

**What procedures make a result trustworthy?**

**When does institutional consensus reflect strong evidence, and when might it merely stabilize a framework?**

The critical distinction is:

> **Authority ≠ truth.**

Expertise matters.

Institutional process matters.

But neither is identical with truth.

---

# 14. Epistemic Standing and Authorization

In ethics, authorization often asks who may intervene in another person's life.

In inquiry, the analogous question is often epistemic:

> **Who has standing to make, certify, interpret, or act upon this claim?**

A scientist may be qualified to interpret a dataset but not to make a policy decision.

A historian may responsibly reconstruct a past episode without claiming access to every actor's inner experience.

A statistical result may justify a research conclusion without authorizing a public claim stronger than the evidence supports.

So even here:

> **Supported judgment ≠ unrestricted authorization.**

This matters whenever inquiry crosses into policy, medicine, engineering, public communication, education, or institutional decision-making.

---

# 15. Use Claim Ceilings

One of the simplest habits in this course is:

> **Ask what does not yet follow.**

Examples:

**Map ≠ World.**

**Evidence ≠ stronger conclusion than it supports.**

**Model success ≠ ontology.**

**Origin ≠ validity.**

**Probe result ≠ historical fact.**

**Authority ≠ truth.**

**Shared grammar ≠ shared ontology.**

**Current judgment ≠ final closure.**

Claim ceilings do not weaken inquiry.

They protect its precision.

---

# 16. REOPEN

Scientific knowledge is often strong precisely because it can be revised.

REOPEN asks:

> **What would make us return this settlement to inquiry?**

A failed prediction?

A new instrument?

An unexplained anomaly?

A better measurement?

A rival model with greater explanatory or predictive success?

A conceptual inconsistency?

A replication failure?

A new archive or primary source?

A changed estimate of uncertainty?

A theory that succeeds in a domain where the current framework fails?

REOPEN does not mean that every established result is perpetually doubtful.

It means that confidence remains connected to conditions of warrant.

The question is not:

> “Can I imagine being wrong?”

It is:

> **What kind of evidence or failure would actually matter?**

---

# 17. Source Ledgers Matter in Historical Inquiry

The history of inquiry has a special danger: later interpretation can easily be mistaken for what historical actors themselves knew or meant.

So this course distinguishes:

**Primary historical source**

**Scholarly reconstruction**

**Contemporary scientific source**

**FSP reconstruction**

Those are not interchangeable.

A historical letter is not a modern scholarly interpretation.

A modern historian's reconstruction is not the actor's own perspective.

An FSP case encoding is not evidence that historical investigators thought in FSP categories.

So we preserve:

> **Historical source ≠ scholarly reconstruction ≠ FSP reconstruction.**

This is both a historical and a critical-thinking discipline.

---

# 18. A Compact Inquiry Routine

When you encounter a historical scientific case, begin with six questions.

### 1. What was observed or made measurable?
What entered the inquiry, through which instrument or representation?

### 2. What inference was licensed?
What conclusion was supported, and what did not yet follow?

### 3. What alternatives remained?
What other explanations, assumptions, or models were still live?

### 4. What shaped the inquiry?
Which concepts, instruments, institutions, practices, or background commitments mattered?

### 5. What is the claim ceiling?
How far can the evidence responsibly carry us?

### 6. What would REOPEN the case?
What anomaly, new evidence, failed prediction, conceptual shift, or better explanation would matter?

That routine is enough to begin.

The historical episodes will make it more precise.

---

# 19. What This Framework Does Not Claim

This framework does not claim that science follows one universal method.

It does not claim that every scientific revolution has the same structure.

It does not claim that every anomaly falsifies a theory.

It does not claim that every successful model reveals final ontology.

It does not claim that Kuhn, Duhem, Quine, Galileo, Einstein, or any other historical figure anticipated FSP.

It does not claim that evidence is arbitrary because interpretation is involved.

It does not claim that institutional consensus is worthless.

It does not replace disciplinary expertise, mathematics, experimentation, historical scholarship, or source criticism.

It does not turn a Tool into a verdict.

It does not make present-day categories identical with historical actors' own concepts.

Its narrower purpose is:

> **to help us inspect how inquiry moves from encounter to representation, from evidence to judgment, from judgment to institutional standing, and from settled expectation to revision—while preserving the claim ceilings that keep those transitions responsible.**

---

# 20. Course Return

The history of inquiry can now be read as more than a sequence of discoveries.

It is also a record of humans learning new ways to make the world answer back.

Galileo makes mathematical and instrument-mediated comparison newly powerful.

Foucault makes precision measurement and theory discrimination visible.

Neptune and Vulcan make auxiliary assumptions and differential theoretical repair visible.

Newton and Einstein make domain, approximation, and conceptual transformation visible.

Duhem and Quine make evidential holism visible.

Kuhn makes disciplinary frameworks and meaning change visible.

Thought experiments make controlled conceptual revision visible.

DESI makes live, collaborative, data-rich frontier inquiry visible.

None of these episodes supplies a final method for every future inquiry.

Each expands what we can notice.

The governing question for the course is therefore not only:

> **Which theory won?**

It is also:

> **What changed in what investigators could observe, represent, infer, compare, explain, test, authorize, and revise—and what does that history teach us about how inquiry should be governed now?**

That question carries the history of inquiry back into present Doing.

## Projection claim ceiling

This introduction is a pedagogical projection of the frozen FSP critical-thinking architecture into a History of Inquiry course.

It does not redefine the canonical Tools, failure modes, or claim ceilings.

It does not establish one universal scientific method.

It does not collapse historical episodes into one philosophy of science.

It does not imply that historical actors used FSP concepts.

Its bounded claim is:

> **The history of inquiry can function not only as a history of changing theories and discoveries, but also as a cumulative repertoire of ways human beings have learned to observe, represent, measure, compare, explain, test, institutionalize, revise, and reopen knowledge claims. Governed critical thinking gives students a shared grammar for carrying those gains forward while preserving the differences among scientific domains, historical periods, evidential practices, and conceptual frameworks.**
