# Thinking Ethically as a Situated Human Inquirer

Ethics does not begin when someone opens a philosophy book.

Long before we encounter Socrates, Aristotle, Aquinas, Hume, Kant, Mill, Marx, Nietzsche, Rawls, or contemporary theories of care and capability, we are already trying to find our way through situations involving other people, expectations, rules, uncertainty, consequences, loyalties, disagreements, institutions, and unequal power.

We notice some things and miss others. We interpret what people meant. We decide which facts matter. We inherit habits and expectations. We make judgments before we know everything. We act, discover consequences, and sometimes revise what we thought.

In that broad sense, ordinary human life already has the structure of inquiry.

This course asks what happens when human beings begin to examine that inquiry deliberately.

Ethics therefore involves more than asking, “What should I do?” It also asks:

- What situation am I actually responding to?
- What have I noticed, and what have I left out?
- What reasons support my judgment?
- How were my habits and expectations formed?
- What was really within someone's control?
- Why should this rule or authority bind?
- Whose perspective is represented?
- Who benefits, who bears the burden, and how certain are we?
- What conditions shaped the available choices?
- Are formally equal rules fair under unequal conditions?
- What can people actually do with the resources and opportunities available to them?
- What would make me revise my current judgment?

The historical philosophers in this book will not all answer these questions in the same way. That disagreement is part of the course.

The purpose of the critical-thinking framework is not to make them agree. It is to help us see more clearly **what kind of ethical work each argument, distinction, or case is asking us to do**.

---

# 1. We Begin with Partial Maps

No one encounters an ethical situation from nowhere.

We enter situations with limited attention, prior experience, expectations, concepts, emotions, social roles, relationships, and histories. We may know some facts but not others. We may hear one person's account before another's. A rule may already frame the problem before we have asked whether the framing is adequate.

For this course, we will call any selective articulation of a situation a **map**.

A map can be a description, story, category, argument, rule, statistic, memory, institutional record, or interpretation. Maps can be very useful. But they remain maps.

The first recurring firewall is:

> **Map ≠ World ≠ Person.**

A description of another person is not that person. A diagnosis is not the whole person. A manager's account of an employee is not the employee. A historical reconstruction is not identical with what the historical actor experienced. A moral theory is not the whole situation to which it is being applied.

So one of our first ethical habits will be simple:

> **Before reasoning confidently from a situation, inspect the map.**

Ask:

**What happened? What am I adding? What might I be missing?**

That question will return throughout the course.

---

# 2. Ethical Thinking Has an Assembly History

The way a situation appears to us has a history.

We learn from families, communities, languages, schools, religious and political traditions, professions, technologies, institutions, rewards, punishments, failures, successes, and relationships. We develop habits of attention. Some responses become familiar. Some distinctions become easy to see. Others remain nearly invisible.

But we are not passive products of those histories. We also resist, revise, reinterpret, practice, explore, combine, and reject what we inherit.

We will call this reciprocal process an **assembly history**.

Assembly history matters because ethical judgment is not made by a blank observer. Our present ways of noticing and valuing have been formed.

But this gives us another important firewall:

> **Formation ≠ justification.**

Knowing how a belief or value arose does not by itself prove that it is right or wrong.

Likewise:

> **Origin ≠ validity.**

A belief with an unpleasant history might still be true. A belief with an admirable history might still be false. Historical explanation can deepen ethical inquiry without replacing the need for reasons.

This distinction will become especially important when we reach Aristotle's account of character formation and later Marx and Nietzsche's investigations of background conditions, values, and power.

---

# 3. Reflection Makes Our Own Inquiry Inspectable

Much of human life proceeds without our stopping to analyze every perception, habit, or response.

Critical thinking becomes more explicit when we can turn back toward our own inquiry and ask questions such as:

**What did I actually observe?**

**What did I infer?**

**Why do I believe this?**

**What reasons support the conclusion?**

**What else might explain what happened?**

**What would count against my current view?**

**How confident should I be?**

This is where familiar critical-thinking skills remain essential.

We will still reconstruct arguments, distinguish premises from conclusions, examine deduction and induction, compare explanations, use analogy, identify fallacies, inspect causal claims, evaluate evidence, consider objections, and revise conclusions.

FSP does not replace those skills.

It extends them.

Why is an extension needed? Because a person can reason very carefully from an incomplete map. A strong argument can support a judgment without settling who may act on that judgment. A policy can produce benefits while distributing serious burdens unfairly. An institution can enforce a rule without making that rule morally legitimate.

So the course will ask us to inspect not only arguments but also the transitions surrounding them.

---

# 4. From Critical Thinking to Governed Ethical Inquiry

A useful way to picture ethical inquiry is:

```text
lived situation
↔ partial map
↔ reasons and evidence
↔ alternatives
↔ bounded judgment
↔ standing / authorization
↔ action
↔ consequences
↔ repair / REOPEN
```

This is not a formula that every ethical problem follows step by step. Real inquiry loops, overlaps, and doubles back.

The purpose of the map is diagnostic.

It helps us ask:

> **What kind of work is needed here?**

Sometimes the main problem is an incomplete representation.

Sometimes the problem is a weak reason.

Sometimes we have confused power with moral authority.

Sometimes we have ignored constraint when assigning responsibility.

Sometimes a person is being helped in a way that bypasses their agency.

Sometimes aggregate benefit hides who bears the burden.

Sometimes a choice seems voluntary only because we have ignored the conditions shaping the available options.

Sometimes our conclusion is reasonable but our proposed intervention is not yet authorized.

Sometimes the most important question is what would make us reopen the judgment later.

This is what this book means by **governed critical thinking**.

# 5. Twelve Recurring Ethical Questions

Across the twelve historical chapters, you will meet twelve recurring inquiry functions.

They are called **Tools**, but they are not formulas that produce answers. They are questions that help expose a different vulnerability in ethical reasoning.

> **Tool ≠ Verdict.**

You do not need to memorize all twelve before beginning the course. Each chapter will develop one Tool in context.

## Attention

**What happened? What am I adding? What am I failing to notice?**

This asks whether the first map of a situation is adequate.

## Examination and Reason-Giving

**What is the claim? What reasons support it? What follows, and what does not?**

This asks whether reasons actually do the work assigned to them.

## Formation and Practical Wisdom

**What is this practice forming people to notice, enjoy, avoid, choose, and become?**

This asks how habits and practices shape practical judgment.

## Control and Moral Range

**What is controllable, influenceable, or outside control?**

This asks how responsibility should be calibrated under constraint.

## Moral Authority and Common Good

**Why should this rule bind?**

This distinguishes enforceability from moral authority.

## Legitimacy and Standing

**Why may this person or institution exercise consequential authority here?**

This asks who may decide, impose, constrain, or intervene.

## Perspective and Moral Visibility

**Whose situation is being represented, by whom, and with what limits?**

This asks how well another person's situation has entered the map.

## Respect, Duty, and Autonomy

**Does this action preserve the person as an agent rather than merely manage them?**

This asks whether intervention respects agency.

## Consequence, Welfare, and Harm

**Who is affected, what happens, how certain are we, and who bears the burden?**

This asks us to inspect effects and distribution.

## Background Power and Genealogy

**What is shaping the choice before the choice appears?**

This asks what structures, histories, incentives, and inherited categories shape the field of action.

## Fair Structure

**Would I choose these rules without knowing which position would be mine?**

This asks us to inspect institutional fairness without privileging our current location.

## Dependency and Effective Capability

**What can this person actually do and be under these conditions?**

This distinguishes formal resources from real possibilities.

These Tools overlap. A difficult case may require several.

The important habit is not memorizing the names. It is learning to notice **which question the situation is demanding**.

---

# 6. Historical Philosophers Are Not Tool Labels

The Tools are modern FSP reconstructions for use in this course.

They should not be confused with the philosophers themselves.

Socrates did not possess an “FSP Examination Tool.” Aristotle did not formulate an “FSP Formation Audit.” Kant did not design an “Agency Bypass” diagnostic. Rawls did not write a Tool card.

Instead, each historical chapter gives us an opportunity to recover a philosophical problem, argument, practice, or distinction in its own setting and then ask what transferable inquiry skill it can help us practice now.

The relationship is therefore:

```text
historical philosopher / text
→ historical problem and argument
→ reconstructed inquiry function
→ contemporary case
→ bounded transfer
```

This protects both sides.

We do not flatten the history of philosophy into FSP.

And we do not treat historical philosophy as something to memorize and leave in the past.

# 7. The Twelve-Chapter Ethical Inquiry Arc

The chapters gradually broaden the kinds of ethical inquiry we can perform.

## Chapter 1 — Attention

Before judging, inspect the situation.

The first lesson is that a mistaken or incomplete map can corrupt everything that follows.

## Chapter 2 — Socrates and Plato

Ask for reasons.

When a claim appeals to authority, tradition, or confidence, ask what is supposed to explain what.

A rule is not just because an authority approved it merely by virtue of that approval.

But showing that one justification fails does not prove the opposite conclusion.

## Chapter 3 — Aristotle

Ethical judgment depends partly on what habits and practices have formed us to notice.

Practical wisdom is not simply applying a rule mechanically.

But formation does not itself settle justification.

## Chapter 4 — Hellenistic Ethics

Agency must be calibrated under constraint.

The fact that some freedom remains does not imply unlimited responsibility.

## Chapter 5 — Aquinas and the Problem of Moral Authority

Rules and institutions may pursue genuine goods while still requiring justification.

A good purpose does not prove that one particular rule is uniquely justified.

## Chapter 6 — Social Contract and Legitimacy

Power and legitimacy are different.

The fact that an institution can compel obedience does not by itself show that it has moral standing.

Likewise, identifying illegitimacy does not automatically authorize every form of resistance.

## Chapter 7 — Hume and Smith

Ethical judgment requires attention to the perspectives of others.

Sympathy and imaginative perspective-taking can enlarge moral visibility.

But:

> **Sympathy ≠ direct access.**

Our map of another person's experience remains a map.

## Chapter 8 — Kant

Respect requires treating persons as agents rather than merely as objects to manage.

But respecting autonomy does not mean that every present preference automatically decides every ethical question.

## Chapter 9 — Bentham and Mill

Consequences matter.

So do uncertainty, distribution, liberty, and the burdens imposed in producing aggregate benefits.

A beneficial outcome is not a complete justification by itself.

## Chapter 10 — Marx and Nietzsche

Choices, values, categories, and moral expectations have histories.

Power and social structure can shape what appears possible before a person chooses.

But again:

> **Origin ≠ validity.**

Explaining where a value came from does not automatically refute it.

## Chapter 11 — Rawls

A formally identical rule may affect people differently because they occupy different starting positions.

Fairness therefore requires inspecting structure, not merely procedural sameness.

## Chapter 12 — Care, Capability, and Maathai

Resources and formal opportunities matter, but they do not guarantee real ability to act.

The ethical question becomes:

> **What can people actually do and be under these conditions?**

The course therefore ends by returning ethical judgment to lived human capability, relationship, cooperation, and institutional conditions.

# 8. Cases Are Not Illustrations After the Philosophy

The real-world cases in this course are not decorations added after the philosophical material.

They are places where ethical inquiry becomes difficult enough that the Tools matter.

Boisjoly and Challenger ask what happens when professional judgment, organizational authority, habits, and risk interact.

Saad Sirop Hanna asks how agency and responsibility should be understood under extreme coercion.

Mandela asks about legitimacy, internal and external strategy, resistance, and the limits of authority.

The Gulf Pines cases ask about perspective, responsibility, welfare, liberty, uncertainty, and institutional action.

The FlexWorks case asks how apparently individual choices may be shaped by background structures and value histories.

Commonwealth Colleges asks whether neutral-looking institutional rules remain fair across unequal positions.

Maathai and the Green Belt Movement ask about dependency, capability, shared action, cooperation without complete agreement, and durable institutional change.

In each case, the goal is not merely to choose the “right answer.”

The goal is to improve the inquiry.

---

# 9. Judgment Is Not the Same as Authorization

This distinction will become increasingly important as the course moves from personal conduct to institutions and public action.

Suppose the evidence supports a conclusion.

Another question remains:

> **What does that conclusion authorize anyone to do?**

A physician may have good evidence but still need consent.

A manager may identify a real problem but lack moral standing to impose a particular solution.

A government may predict a benefit while still needing to justify coercion.

A teacher may reasonably judge that a student needs help while still needing to preserve the student's agency.

So the course will repeatedly distinguish:

> **Supported judgment ≠ unrestricted authorization.**

and:

> **Possibility ≠ Recommendation ≠ Authorization.**

Generating an option does not recommend it.

Recommending an option does not establish who may implement it.

Ethical reasoning therefore extends beyond belief into standing, legitimacy, responsibility, and action.

---

# 10. Institutions Can Amplify Both Good and Bad Inquiry

Ethics does not remain inside individuals.

Judgments become rules, rankings, policies, incentives, diagnoses, procedures, records, technologies, and institutional routines.

Institutions can preserve expertise, coordinate action, and make large-scale cooperation possible.

They can also amplify omission, distance decision makers from consequences, harden provisional categories, stabilize unequal power, and make inherited assumptions difficult to notice.

So when a judgment becomes institutional, additional questions arise:

**Which map became operational?**

**Who can challenge it?**

**Which voices count as evidence?**

**Who bears the burdens?**

**Who has standing to revise the rule?**

**What would trigger repair?**

One of the central movements of this course is therefore from individual moral judgment toward **connected Doing**: people acting through relationships, practices, organizations, and institutions.

---

# 11. Use Claim Ceilings

One of the simplest habits in this course is to ask:

> **What does not yet follow?**

A claim ceiling prevents a useful insight from becoming an unlimited conclusion.

Examples:

**Better map ≠ final judgment.**

**Failed justification ≠ opposite conclusion proved.**

**Formation ≠ justification.**

**Remaining agency ≠ unlimited responsibility.**

**Power ≠ legitimacy.**

**Sympathy ≠ direct access.**

**Benefit ≠ complete justification.**

**Origin ≠ validity.**

**Resource ≠ capability.**

**Current judgment ≠ final closure.**

Claim ceilings are not excuses for indecision.

They are ways of being precise about what we have earned.

---

# 12. REOPEN

Ethical judgment is often made under uncertainty.

We still have to act.

But action does not have to mean permanent closure.

**REOPEN** asks:

> **What would make us reconsider this judgment?**

New evidence?

A person previously left out?

Unexpected harm?

A failed prediction?

A challenge to authority?

A change in circumstances?

A better explanation?

Evidence that a repair created a new problem?

REOPEN turns intellectual humility into a practical discipline.

The goal is not endless hesitation.

The goal is a judgment strong enough to guide action and bounded enough to remain corrigible.

---

# 13. Your Role: Authorial Return

The purpose of the course is not to hand your judgment over to a philosopher, a theory, a Tool, a simulation, an institution, an instructor, or an AI system.

Those resources can help you inspect a problem.

They can expose distinctions, recover arguments, compare possibilities, identify missing evidence, and pressure-test conclusions.

But the course repeatedly returns judgment to you.

This is **Authorial Return**.

> **Cognitive support ≠ outsourced authorship.**

A strong final judgment should therefore be something you can explain in your own words:

- what you think;
- why you think it;
- what evidence or reasons matter;
- what remains uncertain;
- what the judgment does and does not authorize;
- and what would make you reopen it.

That is the form of ethical authorship this course is designed to develop.

---

# 14. A Compact Ethical Inquiry Routine

When a difficult ethical case appears, you do not need to run all twelve Tools.

Start with six questions:

### 1. What is my map?
What happened, what am I adding, and what might be missing?

### 2. What are the reasons?
What supports the judgment, and what actually follows?

### 3. What shaped the situation?
What habits, constraints, institutions, histories, or power relations matter?

### 4. Who is affected and who has standing?
Whose perspective is represented? Who may decide or intervene?

### 5. What is my claim ceiling?
What does not yet follow from the evidence or Tool I used?

### 6. What would REOPEN this?
What new evidence, consequence, challenge, or change would make me reconsider?

That compact routine is enough to begin.

The historical chapters will deepen it.

---

# 15. What This Framework Does Not Claim

This framework does not claim that every moral problem has one correct algorithmic answer.

It does not claim that the twelve historical traditions secretly share one ethical theory.

It does not replace logic, evidence, historical interpretation, or careful reading.

It does not turn a Tool into a verdict.

It does not treat an FSP reconstruction as identical with what a philosopher historically believed.

It does not claim that understanding the origin of a value decides its validity.

It does not claim that every supported judgment authorizes intervention.

It does not claim that a current conclusion is permanently closed.

Its more modest purpose is this:

> **to help us notice where ethical inquiry can go wrong, select a useful question for repairing it, make a bounded judgment, and remain able to revise that judgment when the world, other people, or consequences give us reason to do so.**

---

# 16. Course Return

The history of ethics can now be read as more than a sequence of theories.

It can also be read as a long record of human beings discovering new things that ethical inquiry may need to notice.

Socrates makes reason-giving harder to evade.

Aristotle makes formation and practical judgment visible.

Hellenistic ethics makes constraint and moral range visible.

Aquinas makes the problem of ordered goods and moral authority visible.

Social-contract traditions make legitimacy visible.

Hume and Smith make perspective and sympathy visible.

Kant makes respect and agency visible.

Bentham and Mill make consequence, welfare, liberty, and distribution visible.

Marx and Nietzsche make background power, genealogy, and value-history visible.

Rawls makes fair structure visible.

Care and capability approaches make dependency, relationship, and effective human possibility visible.

None of these discoveries ends ethical inquiry.

Each expands what we can notice.

The governing question for the course is therefore not only:

> **Which philosopher is right?**

It is also:

> **What does this philosopher help us see that we might otherwise miss, what follows from seeing it, what does not yet follow, and how should that insight change the way we inquire and act?**

That question carries the history of ethics back into lived Doing.

## Projection claim ceiling

This introduction is a pedagogical projection of the frozen FSP critical-thinking architecture into a History of Ethics course.

It does not redefine the canonical Tools, failure modes, or claim ceilings.

It does not establish that historical philosophers anticipated FSP.

It does not collapse ethical traditions into one theory.

Its bounded claim is:

> **The history of ethics can function not only as a history of competing answers, but also as a cumulative repertoire of ways human beings have learned to inspect moral situations, reasons, formation, agency, authority, perspective, consequence, power, fairness, capability, and revision. Governed critical thinking gives students a common grammar for carrying those gains forward without erasing the differences among the traditions that produced them.**
