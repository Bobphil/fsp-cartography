# FSP-BRANDOM-RECIPROCITY-1

## Phenomenal Node vs Discursive Scorekeeper, Commitment/Entitlement Crosswalk, Reciprocal Recognition, Standing and Moral-Standing Firewall, Stag-Hunt Cooperation, Institutional Sedimentation, Historical Recollection, and Full-Stack Extension Test

**Status:** COMPARATOR ARCHITECTURE — VERSION 1  
**Purpose:** Test whether Robert Brandom's inferentialist and recognitive account of normativity can be incorporated into Full-Stack Philosophy without collapsing phenomenal life, moral standing, institutional power, or historical sedimentation into discursive scorekeeping.  
**Comparator posture:** Selective inheritance, explicit resistance, and full-stack extension.  
**Primary claim ceiling:** Strong structural fit between Brandomian normative pragmatics and FSP interpersonal governance ≠ identity of frameworks or proof that Brandom supplies a complete ethics.

---

# 1. Why Brandom is a serious comparator

Brandom offers one of the most developed accounts of normativity as a social practice of commitments, entitlements, authority, responsibility, and reciprocal recognition.

His inferentialism explains conceptual content through what follows from a commitment, what supports it, and what is incompatible with it.

His social pragmatics explains discursive practice through participants keeping track of what they and others are committed and entitled to.

His Hegelian account of recognition explains normative status as socially instituted through reciprocal attribution of authority and responsibility.

These ideas intersect directly with several FSP commitments:

- claim / warrant separation;
- standing;
- authority;
- responsibility;
- interpersonal representation;
- challenge;
- licensing;
- translation;
- social coordination;
- sedimentation;
- historical reconstruction.

But the fit is not complete.

FSP begins from lived Doing and phenomenal human worlds before discursive articulation.

It also distinguishes socially attributed status from moral standing and legitimate authorization.

And it tracks how normative practices become institutions, technologies, infrastructures, and future assembly conditions.

So Brandom is best treated as a powerful **middle-layer comparator** inside a larger FSP ecology.

## Level 1 — Phenomenal human node

```text
lived phenomenal world

```

This includes:

- bodily experience;
- affect;
- memory;
- attention;
- science;
- religion;
- culture;
- practical competence;
- aspiration;
- Beauty / Awe / Mattering;
- material capability.

Brandom's framework does not primarily operate here.

## Level 2 — Interpersonal normative practice

```text
person
↔ representation of other
↔ recognition
↔ claim
↔ commitment
↔ entitlement
↔ challenge
↔ reasons
↔ standing
↔ shared task
```

This is where Brandom is strongest.

## Level 3 — Historical / institutional ecology

```text

→ repeated coordination
→ norm
→ institution
→ artifact / infrastructure
→ sedimentation
→ future assembly conditions
```

Brandom's Hegelian historicity reaches into this level.

FSP extends it more explicitly into:

- material infrastructure;
- institutional amplification;
- capability effects;
- social equilibria;
- future-person burden;
- ecological consequences;
- REOPEN of sedimented systems.

# 3. Phenomenal node vs discursive scorekeeper

A Brandomian participant is a discursive scorekeeper.

They track:

- acknowledged commitments;
- attributed commitments;
- entitlements;
- incompatibilities;
- consequences;
- authority;
- responsibility.

An FSP phenomenal node is broader.

It includes all of that when discursive practice is present, but also includes:

- unarticulated affect;
- bodily condition;
- tacit skill;
- aesthetic attraction;
- grief;
- fear;
- attachment;
- non-discursive salience;
- inherited cultural expectation;
- material constraint;
- capability.

So:

> **Discursive scorekeeper ⊂ phenomenal-node interpretation**

as a functional comparison, not a metaphysical containment claim.

The important Firewall is:

> **Discursive articulation ≠ exhaustive phenomenal significance.**

A person can be morally affected before they can articulate the relevant commitment.

A person can suffer before they can supply reasons.

A person can matter before they can participate in scorekeeping.

| Brandom | FSP analogue | FSP extension |
|---|---|---|
| commitment | undertaken claim / responsibility | source, provenance, downstream authorization effects |
| entitlement | warrant / licensed commitment | claim ceiling, evidence state, standing |
| incompatibility | blocked or unstable commitment relation | Firewall / failure pressure |

The crosswalk is strong enough to support interoperability.

It is not strong enough to justify conceptual identity.

> **Crosswalk ≠ reduction.**

# 5. Deontic scorekeeping as a special map

FSP can interpret Brandomian scorekeeping as one important kind of interpersonal map.

Person A may maintain:

```text
A's self-map
A's world-map
A's map of B
A's attribution of B's commitments
A's attribution of B's entitlements
A's attribution of B's authority
```

Person B maintains a different set.

The maps need not agree.

So:

> **A's score of B ≠ B's self-understanding.**

And:

> **Attributed commitment ≠ acknowledged commitment.**

This fits directly with FSP's distinction between:

- said;
- inferred;
- self-attributed;
- other-attributed;
- provenance.

Brandom therefore gives FSP a rich normative interpretation of interpersonal map-tracking.

---

# 6. Commitment and entitlement strengthen the Firewall concept

FSP says:

> **You may cross, but show the license.**

Brandom helps specify one important type of license.

A commitment may be inferentially connected to another commitment.

But a participant must also be entitled to the starting commitment and preserve entitlement through the inference.

This suggests a useful distinction.

## Inferential Firewall

Does the conclusion follow?

## Entitlement Firewall

Is the participant entitled to the premises or commitment?

## Standing Firewall

Is the participant in a position to recommend, authorize, or impose what follows?

## Institutional Firewall

What happens when the commitment becomes rule, policy, default, or infrastructure?

Brandom is especially strong at the first two.

FSP extends the architecture through the latter two.

---

# 7. Recognition as normative status

Brandom's Hegelian interpretation treats recognition as attribution of normative authority and responsibility.

To recognize someone is to treat them as a subject who can:

- undertake commitments;
- exercise authority;
- be held responsible;
- assess others;
- participate in the social space of reasons.

Reciprocal recognition occurs when recognitive authority is mutually attributed.

This strongly supports FSP's emerging reciprocity core.

A person is not merely a source of data.

They are a participant whose perspective can make claims on others.

---

# 8. The moral-standing problem

FSP cannot identify socially recognized normative status with complete moral standing.

History and the present contain many communities in which some persons are denied recognition as full moral beings.

Examples include structures organized around:

- slavery;
- caste;
- racial supremacy;
- misogyny;
- religious exclusion;
- ethnic domination;
- dehumanizing nationalism;
- disability exclusion;
- colonial hierarchy.

Participants inside such systems may reciprocally recognize one another.

They may keep sophisticated deontic score.

They may undertake commitments.

They may build trust.

They may cooperate effectively.

They may create institutions.

They may even regard the system as normatively binding.

Yet those practices can deny standing to others.

Therefore:

> **Socially attributed normative status ≠ exhaustive moral standing.**

This is a major FSP Firewall.

---

# 9. Four kinds of standing

The comparator therefore distinguishes at least four kinds of standing.

## Attributed standing

What another participant treats you as entitled or authorized to do.

## Institutional standing

What a rule, office, law, procedure, or institution recognizes.

## Effective standing

What you can actually exercise in practice.

## Moral standing

What standing ought to be recognized under the relevant ethical governance.

These can diverge.

A person may possess moral standing while lacking institutional standing.

A person may possess institutional standing while exercising illegitimate power.

A person may be formally entitled to speak but lack effective standing because of coercion or incapacity.

This gives FSP a richer recognitive grammar than simple social attribution.

---

# 10. Representation without endorsement

FSP must be able to represent morally exclusionary PRFs accurately.

Suppose a group holds:

```text
PRF-X:
members of group X possess full moral status
members of group Y do not
```

FSP should record that perspective.

It should represent:

- what members believe;
- what evidence they cite;
- what authority they recognize;
- what commitments follow;
- how they coordinate;
- how they treat outsiders;
- what institutions they build;
- what consequences result.

But:

> **Perspective representation ≠ normative endorsement.**

And:

> **Represented commitment ≠ licensed FSP commitment.**

The FSP moral-standing Firewall may reject the transition from:

```text
our group does not recognize Y
```

to:

```text
Y therefore lacks moral standing
```

unless an adequate warrant can be supplied.

---

# 11. Competing Joint Task Spaces

Morally exclusionary groups may still form effective task spaces.

They may:

- coordinate politically;
- build institutions;
- distribute resources;
- create propaganda;
- enforce norms;
- compete for power;
- construct economic systems;
- organize violence;
- build cultural narratives.

Their internal Joint Task Space may be highly functional.

This means:

> **Functional Joint Task Space ≠ legitimate Joint Task Space.**

FSP must represent both:

## Internal coordination success

How successfully does the group coordinate?

## External normative status

What burdens does its coordination place on others?

Whose standing is denied?

What authority is claimed?

What harms are produced?

What is sedimented?

This distinction is essential for modeling conflict among groups.

---

# 12. Social competition among task spaces

A society can contain multiple partially overlapping task spaces.

Some cooperate.

Some compete.

Some dominate.

Some attempt to reform others.

Some refuse recognition.

A dynamic model may contain:

```text
JTS-A
↔ competition
JTS-B
↔ coalition
JTS-C
↔ conflict
JTS-D
```

Each may contain different:

- PRFs;
- norms;
- recognition structures;
- institutions;
- resource bases;
- narratives;
- technologies;
- power.

Social history can then be understood partly as interaction among competing coordination systems.

This gives FSP a way to represent conflict without pretending that everyone already shares one moral world.

---

# 13. Reciprocity can be local and exclusionary

Reciprocity itself is not sufficient for justice.

A group may practice strong internal reciprocity while excluding outsiders.

Members may:

- trust one another;
- recognize one another;
- honor commitments;
- distribute responsibility;
- punish defection.

Yet the group may coordinate around domination.

So:

> **Internal reciprocity ≠ universal moral reciprocity.**

This is another important Firewall.

Brandomian reciprocal recognition can explain the internal normative stability of a group.

FSP must additionally ask:

> Who is outside the circle of recognition?

> Who bears the effects?

> Who has standing to contest the task?

---

# 14. Stag Hunts and normative cooperation

Brandom helps enrich the Stag Hunt model.

Human cooperation requires more than aligned payoff.

It often requires:

- assurances;
- commitments;
- entitlement to rely on others;
- recognized authority;
- responsibility;
- sanctions;
- trust.

Brandom's normative vocabulary provides a microgrammar for these relations.

A cooperative trajectory can be modeled as:

```text
mutual recognition
→ commitment
→ entitlement to rely
→ trust
→ coordinated Doing
→ expanded feasible set
→ Stag Hunt success
```

This helps explain how normativity can make large-scale cooperation possible.

---

# 15. Bad Stag Hunts

The same structure can stabilize harmful cooperation.

A group may coordinate successfully to:

- exclude;
- dominate;
- exploit;
- censor;
- dispossess;
- wage aggressive war;
- degrade an ecosystem.

The task can be successful relative to internal goals.

Therefore:

> **Successful cooperation ≠ legitimate cooperation.**

And:

> **Expanded feasible set ≠ morally improved feasible set.**

This is where Brandomian normativity must be embedded inside broader FSP ethical governance.

---

# 16. Recognition, trust, and equilibrium

Repeated reciprocal recognition can stabilize expectations.

If participants expect others to:

- honor commitments;
- provide reasons;
- accept correction;
- maintain responsibility;

then cooperation can become easier.

This can move a group from a low-trust equilibrium toward a higher-capability equilibrium.

But exclusionary recognition can also stabilize a hostile equilibrium between groups.

So:

```text
recognition structure
→ expectation structure
→ strategy choice
→ interaction pattern
→ sedimented equilibrium
```

Recognition is therefore dynamically consequential.

---

# 17. Normative practice can sediment

A repeated commitment structure can become:

- custom;
- rule;
- profession;
- office;
- law;
- institution;
- curriculum;
- software;
- metric;
- protocol.

This is the key FSP extension.

Brandom explains how normative statuses operate within social practice.

FSP asks what happens when those practices become infrastructure.

```text
recognitive practice
→ repeated norm
→ institutional rule
→ material / technical embedding
→ future constraint
```

This creates the Institutional Amplification problem.

---

# 18. Sedimented recognition can outlive the recognizers

A recognition structure can become institutional even after its original participants die.

For example:

- legal categories;
- inherited property rules;
- professional norms;
- racial classifications;
- bureaucratic procedures;
- educational credentials.

Future persons encounter these as already-existing conditions.

Thus:

> **Current institutional standing may be sedimented past recognition.**

This adds a diachronic layer not captured by immediate interpersonal scorekeeping.

---

# 19. Historical recollection and FSP reconstruction

Brandom's Hegelian account of recollective rationality gives historical development an important role.

Later participants reconstruct earlier commitments as part of a rational trajectory.

FSP strongly shares the importance of backward-looking reconstruction.

But it adds a Firewall:

> **Recollective intelligibility ≠ historical actuality.**

Present reconstruction can reveal:

- dependencies;
- commitments;
- consequences;
- conceptual development.

But it can also over-rationalize history.

FSP therefore requires:

- source provenance;
- historical context;
- alternative reconstruction;
- remainder;
- claim ceilings.

---

# 20. Recollection vs historical trace

The relation can be represented as:

```text
past Doing
→ surviving trace
→ present reconstruction
→ present rational interpretation
```

Brandom emphasizes the rational recollection.

FSP emphasizes that the trace constrains the reconstruction.

So:

> **Present rational reconstruction ≠ recovered past phenomenal world.**

This keeps historical inquiry from becoming retroactive system-building.

---

# 21. Historical Ethics as a recognitive genealogy

The History of Ethics can be reread as transformations in:

- who receives standing;
- what responsibilities are acknowledged;
- who may demand reasons;
- who is recognized as an agent;
- what counts as legitimate authority.

Examples include:

- Socratic reason-giving;
- Aristotle and formed agency;
- social-contract legitimacy;
- Hume/Smith perspective;
- Kantian personhood;
- Millian liberty;
- Marx/Nietzsche formation and power;
- Rawlsian fairness;
- care/capability inclusion.

This is not a Brandomian rewriting of the history.

It is a comparative lens.

---

# 22. Brandom helps specify inferential licensing

FSP Firewalls often take the form:

```text
C1
≠
C2 without additional warrant
```

Brandom gives a strong account of:

- consequential commitment;
- incompatibility;
- entitlement;
- inferential articulation.

This can strengthen FSP's formal treatment of warrants.

A future formalization could distinguish:

```text
commitment relation
entitlement relation
standing relation
authorization relation
```

rather than treating "warrant" as one undifferentiated field.

---

# 23. But entitlement is perspective-dependent

Brandomian scorekeeping is perspectival.

A participant may attribute entitlement that another participant denies.

FSP already has the architecture for this.

For a claim:

```text
speaker acknowledges commitment
scorekeeper attributes commitment
speaker claims entitlement
scorekeeper attributes / denies entitlement
institution assigns standing
FSP audit evaluates warrant
```

These should remain separable.

This is particularly important in plural societies.

---

# 24. FSP adds provenance to scorekeeping

Brandomian scorekeeping can be enriched by FSP provenance.

Instead of merely:

```text
B is committed to p
```

FSP can ask:

- Did B explicitly say p?
- Did A infer p from B's other commitments?
- Which inference rule was used?
- Was the attribution contested?
- What source supports it?
- What was the context?
- Has B revised?
- What remains uncertain?

Thus:

> **Attributed commitment ≠ source-independent fact about the person.**

---

# 25. FSP adds remainder

Not everything relevant to a person's lived world is inferentially articulated.

Remainder may include:

- tacit competence;
- embodied pain;
- unarticulated fear;
- aesthetic significance;
- trauma;
- attachment;
- religious experience;
- identity;
- relational dependence.

So:

> **Inferential content ≠ total human meaning.**

This is one of FSP's clearest extensions beyond Brandom.

---

# 26. FSP adds capability

A person can be normatively entitled to act but unable to act.

A person may be formally recognized but practically silenced.

A person may be legally free but materially incapable.

Therefore:

> **Normative entitlement ≠ effective capability.**

FSP brings capability analysis into the recognitive system.

---

# 27. FSP adds authorization states

Brandom's entitlement language is not identical with FSP authorization.

A participant may be entitled to assert a claim without being authorized to impose a policy.

So:

> **Discursive entitlement ≠ institutional authorization.**

FSP preserves:

```text
GENERATED
→ POSSIBLE
→ RECOMMENDED
→ AUTHORIZED
→ IMPLEMENTED
→ REVIEWED
```

This is essential in professional and institutional settings.

---

# 28. FSP adds future-person standing

Future persons cannot participate in present reciprocal recognition.

Yet present actions can impose large burdens on them.

Therefore FSP cannot make current reciprocal recognition a necessary condition for moral standing.

This is a decisive pressure test.

> **Present recognitive participation ≠ necessary condition of moral standing.**

This applies also to:

- infants;
- persons with profound cognitive disability;
- temporarily incapacitated persons.

This is one reason FSP must keep moral standing partly independent of discursive scorekeeping.

---

# 29. Nonhuman standing remains open

Animals, ecosystems, and perhaps future artificial agents create further pressure.

Brandom's discursive-recognition model is centered on normative subjects capable of commitments and reasons.

FSP's standing architecture must remain capable of representing claims that moral standing extends beyond fully discursive agents.

Current status:

**OPEN QUESTION.**

No automatic extension is asserted.

But:

> **Discursive competence ≠ exhaustive criterion of possible moral standing.**

# 30. Brandomian normativity inside the Joint Task Space

A mature JTS can incorporate Brandomian functions explicitly.

## State

What commitments and claims are currently live?

## Standing

Who has authority to assert, recommend, challenge, authorize?

## Translation

How do commitments and inferential roles travel across participants?

## Repair

Which commitments can be revised without destroying the shared task?

## Amplification

What happens when commitments become institutional rules?

## REOPEN

What new incompatibility, challenge, consequence, or evidence requires revision?

## Return

Who now undertakes responsibility for the next judgment?

Brandom strengthens the normative microstructure of each layer.

# 32. Conflict between recognition systems

Suppose group A recognizes authority X.

Group B rejects X.

The issue cannot always be solved by more inferential articulation inside one shared space of reasons.

There may be:

- incompatible starting commitments;
- different sources of authority;
- different moral circles;
- unequal power.

FSP then shifts from simple shared reasoning to governance of conflict.

This may require:

- standing analysis;
- constitutional Firewalls;
- rights constraints;
- non-domination;
- negotiated task boundaries;
- refusal.

---

# 33. Refusal is part of reciprocity

Cooperation is not always required.

A person may legitimately refuse:

- an unjust task;
- degrading recognition;
- coerced participation;
- a system that denies their standing.

So:

> **Reciprocity ≠ obligation to cooperate.**

And:

> **Recognition ≠ submission.**

This preserves Mandela-style internal/external strategy and resistance inside the broader reciprocity architecture.

# 35. What FSP should inherit from Brandom

FSP should seriously consider inheriting or adapting:

1. commitment / entitlement separation;
2. inferential role as part of conceptual content;
3. incompatibility relations;
4. multiple scorekeeping perspectives;
5. acknowledged vs attributed commitment;
6. reciprocal recognition;
7. authority/responsibility structure;
8. recollective rationality as a comparator for historical reconstruction.

# 36. What FSP should not inherit without qualification

FSP should resist any interpretation that implies:

1. phenomenal significance is exhausted by discursive content;
2. social recognition exhausts moral standing;
3. current recognition communities are self-validating;
4. discursive entitlement equals institutional authorization;
5. all morally relevant beings must be scorekeeping participants;
6. historical recollection can replace source-bounded reconstruction;
7. normativity alone captures material capability or institutional power.

These are comparator Firewalls.

# 38. Brandom and reciprocal care

Brandomian recognition can give reciprocal care a sharper normative structure.

Reciprocal care is not merely:

> I feel concern for you.

It includes:

> I treat you as someone whose claims can demand reasons from me.

> I recognize that you may correct my map of you.

> I accept responsibilities created by our relation.

FSP adds:

> I also recognize that your lived world exceeds what either of us can fully articulate.

So reciprocal care becomes:

```text
recognition
+ reason-giving
+ representational humility
+ standing
+ capability
+ contestability
+ REOPEN
```

# 39. Sedimentation of moral exclusion

Morally exclusionary PRFs can become socially durable.

The path may be:

```text
exclusionary PRF
→ reciprocal recognition within in-group
→ successful internal coordination
→ institutional rule
→ cultural narrative
→ material advantage / disadvantage
→ future assembly histories
```

Later persons may inherit the exclusion without personally reconstructing its original rationale.

This is precisely why historical sedimentation matters.

The system may reproduce moral exclusion after the original recognizers are gone.

---

# 40. Counter-sedimentation

Reform movements can build alternative recognition structures.

They may:

- expand standing;
- create new testimony channels;
- alter laws;
- change institutions;
- revise public language;
- change who is recognized as authoritative;
- build new shared tasks.

Thus:

```text
excluded perspective
→ counter-public / alternative task space
→ challenge
→ coalition
→ institutional reform
→ new sedimentation
```

This gives FSP a way to model moral progress without assuming inevitable convergence.

---

# 41. Brandomian historical recollection and moral progress

Brandom's Hegelian recollection can help explain how later communities reinterpret earlier normative failures as stages in a developing understanding.

FSP can use this cautiously.

A later community may say:

> We now understand why an earlier recognition structure was defective.

But FSP requires:

- historical provenance;
- alternative perspectives;
- evidence of excluded persons;
- material consequences;
- remainder.

So:

> **Retrospective moral intelligibility ≠ inevitable historical progress.**

# 43. Strongest synthesis claim

The current comparison supports:

> **Brandom supplies a powerful microgrammar of discursive normativity through commitments, entitlements, scorekeeping, and reciprocal recognition; FSP can embed that microgrammar within a larger phenomenal, material, institutional, and historical ecology in which persons exceed their discursive articulation, recognition communities may be morally exclusionary, cooperative task spaces may be legitimate or illegitimate, and sedimented norms reshape the worlds inherited by future persons.**

This does not establish that either framework reduces to the other.

---

# 44. REOPEN conditions

REOPEN this comparator if:

- closer Brandom scholarship undermines the standing distinction used here;
- Brandom's account already contains resources that make an FSP extension redundant;
- FSP's moral-standing distinction cannot be justified without importing a full external moral theory;
- commitment/entitlement mapping fails across non-discursive domains;
- recognition proves less central to cooperative task formation than proposed;
- empirical or historical cases show different sedimentation dynamics;
- future-person or nonhuman standing requires a different constitutional basis;
- scorekeeping cannot be represented without distorting PRF architecture.

# 46. Final assessment

Brandom does not need to become the foundation of FSP.

That would make the framework narrower than the phenomenal-node ecology now requires.

But his inferentialism and Hegelian recognition theory can provide an exceptionally powerful account of the **normative interpersonal middle layer**.

The most promising relation is:

```text
FSP phenomenal ecology
contains
Brandomian discursive normativity
which supports
FSP Joint Task Spaces
which generate
institutional and historical dynamics
that feed back into
future phenomenal ecologies
```

The crucial safeguard is that FSP can represent recognition systems that it does not endorse.

A group can be internally coherent.

Its members can reciprocally recognize one another.

They can cooperate successfully.

They can build powerful institutions.

And FSP can still conclude that the group's claims fail a moral-standing, legitimacy, authorization, or capability Firewall.

That capacity is essential for representing the actual social world.

The strongest compact formulation is therefore:

> **Recognition explains how normative communities can form; FSP must additionally govern which recognitions, exclusions, tasks, and institutional consequences are morally and politically licensed.**

> **FSP-BRANDOM-RECIPROCITY-1: COMPARATOR VERSION 1 — STRONG FIT WITH FULL-STACK EXTENSION REQUIRED.**
