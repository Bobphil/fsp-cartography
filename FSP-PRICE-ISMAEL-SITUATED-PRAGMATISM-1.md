# FSP-PRICE-ISMAEL-SITUATED-PRAGMATISM-1

## Subject Naturalism, Self-Location, Perspectivalism, Vocabulary Function, Observer/Agent Separation, Objective–Situated Reconstruction, and Transition Governance

**Status:** JOINT COMPARATOR ARCHITECTURE — VERSION 1  
**Parents:** FSP-BRANDOM-INFERENTIAL-GOVERNANCE-1; FSP-CONSTITUTIONAL-COMPRESSION-1; FSP-CONSTITUTIONAL-REGRESSION-1; FSP-RECOMPOSITION-GOVERNANCE-1  
**Comparators:** Huw Price — subject naturalism, global expressivism, anti-representationalism, pragmatic function of vocabularies; Jenann Ismael — self-location, reflexivity, embedded agency, observer/agent distinction, objective–situated reconstruction  
**Primary purpose:** Test whether Price and Ismael jointly improve FSP’s account of situated inquiry, partial maps, perspective, ontology, translation, causation, agency, and Return without adding new constitutional primitives.  
**Primary claim ceiling:** This build does not make FSP a global expressivist theory, does not adopt Ismael’s specific metaphysics of self, time, causation, or quantum mechanics, and does not claim that all vocabularies are nonrepresentational. It identifies methodological constraints and decompressions that improve transition governance.

# 1. Why Price and Ismael Belong Together

Price and Ismael address different but adjacent problems.

Price asks:

> **Why assume our vocabularies all represent the world in one uniform way?**

Ismael asks:

> **How does a physically embedded, self-locating agent inhabit and act within the world represented by objective science?**

FSP asks:

> **How should transitions among these situated articulations be governed?**

The joint architecture is therefore:

```text
OBJECTIVE / PUBLIC WORLD
↓
EMBEDDED AGENT
↓
SELF-LOCATION / ACCESS / ACTION
↓
VOCABULARY / PRACTICE FUNCTION
↓
DISCursive ARTICULATION
↓
BOUNDARY CROSSING
↓
GOVERNED TRANSITION
↓
CONSEQUENCE
↓
RETURN
```

This is a comparator structure, not a metaphysical hierarchy.

# 2. Price: Subject Naturalism

Price distinguishes **subject naturalism** from familiar object naturalism.

Object naturalism begins by asking:

> What natural objects or properties does this vocabulary represent?

Subject naturalism begins instead with natural agents and their practices:

> What are creatures like us doing with this vocabulary?

This is highly compatible with FSP.

FSP should therefore prefer:

```text
agent / practice
→ vocabulary use
→ function
→ transition role
→ ontology if needed
```

over:

```text
vocabulary
→ assumed represented entity
```

This strengthens FSP’s anti-reification discipline.

---

# 3. Price: Functional Vocabulary First

The Pricean constraint is:

> **Ask what the vocabulary does before asking what it represents.**

For FSP, this can be applied to:

- moral language;
- causal language;
- probabilistic language;
- modal language;
- institutional categories;
- phenomenal vocabulary;
- scientific-model vocabulary.

Thus:

```text
VOCABULARY
↓
FUNCTION IN PRACTICE
↓
RELATIONS IT ENABLES
↓
TRANSITIONS IT LICENSES
↓
CLAIM CEILING
↓
ONTOLOGICAL COMMITMENT, IF REQUIRED
```

This order should remain provisional but is methodologically powerful.

# 4. Anti-Uniform Representationalism

Price’s anti-representationalism does not require FSP to deny representation.

It requires FSP to reject the assumption that all successful vocabularies work through one uniform representational relation.

Thus:

> **Shared usefulness ≠ shared representational relation.**

and:

> **Map portability ≠ semantic uniformity.**

This strengthens the constitutional rule:

> **Map ≠ World ≠ Person.**

---

# 5. i-Representation and e-Representation

Price distinguishes two broad representational attractors.

## i-representation

A state or expression is understood through its role in an inferential or functional network.

## e-representation

A state or expression is understood through correlation or tracking relations to the environment.

FSP should not force these into one type.

Instead:

```text
map function
may depend on:
- inferential role
- environmental tracking
- practical coordination
- action guidance
- institutional classification
- hybrid combinations
```

This is a strong argument against a universal Map ontology.

---

# 6. Price and Brandom

Brandom foregrounds inferential articulation.

Price pressures that uniformity by emphasizing that vocabularies may perform different functions.

FSP should therefore preserve:

```text
BRANDOM:
What follows from this commitment?

PRICE:
What function does this vocabulary/practice serve?

FSP:
Which question is relevant to this transition?
```

Thus:

> **Inferential role ≠ exhaustive vocabulary function.**

# 7. Vocabulary Function as a Decompression Trigger

Price suggests a useful FSP diagnostic.

When a vocabulary becomes philosophically contested, ask:

1. What practice uses it?
2. What coordination or inference does it enable?
3. What would fail if it disappeared?
4. Does its use require representational interpretation?
5. What ontology, if any, is necessary?

This may reduce unnecessary ontology disputes.

---

# 8. Vocabulary Use ≠ Ontological License

The Pricean firewall is:

> **Vocabulary use ≠ ontological license.**

Examples:

- using “cause” does not by itself settle the metaphysics of causation;
- using “value” does not settle the ontology of values;
- using “remainder” does not imply a natural kind called Remainder;
- using “standing” does not imply one metaphysical property shared across all domains.

This strongly reinforces FSP’s No Decorative Type and No Orphan Distinction rules.

# 9. Ismael: Self-Location

Ismael’s work on the situated self introduces a distinct problem.

An agent may possess a world model without yet locating itself within that model.

Self-location is the relation:

```text
WORLD MODEL
+
INDEX OF SELF
→
SITUATED MODEL
```

The “you are here” structure is not merely a metaphor.

It identifies the agent’s place in the represented environment.

This matters because action depends on location.

---

# 10. Self-Location Is Not Just Another Fact

A self-locating fact has a special practical role.

Knowing:

```text
The exit is 50 meters north of point X
```

differs from knowing:

```text
I am at point X
```

when action depends on orientation.

Thus:

> **World description ≠ self-located world description.**

This is especially important for FSP’s Transformation and Capability architectures.

# 12. Same World Model ≠ Same Situated Significance

Two agents may agree on the public facts while differing in:

- location;
- access;
- capability;
- institutional role;
- vulnerability;
- practical options;
- temporal horizon.

Therefore:

> **Shared world model ≠ shared situated significance.**

This improves FSP’s treatment of perspective.

# 13. Perspective Must Be Typed

“Perspective” is too broad if left undifferentiated.

The Price–Ismael synthesis suggests at least the following dimensions:

```text
ACCESS PERSPECTIVE
What can be detected or known?

SELF-LOCATING PERSPECTIVE
Where is the agent in the map?

AGENTIVE PERSPECTIVE
What can the agent alter from here?

REPRESENTATIONAL PERSPECTIVE
How is the situation articulated?

PRAGMATIC PERSPECTIVE
Why does this vocabulary matter in practice?

NORMATIVE / INSTITUTIONAL PERSPECTIVE
What standing, role, authority, or obligation applies?
```

These should remain dimensions, not six new canonical objects.

---

# 14. Perspective Type ≠ Ontology

A typed perspective does not imply six kinds of world.

It identifies six ways in which a transition may depend on the agent’s situation.

Firewall:

> **Perspective typing ≠ ontological partition.**

# 15. Ismael: Observer vs Agent

Ismael’s recent work distinguishes observational and agentive perspectives.

This is especially important for FSP.

An observer asks:

> What can be registered or known?

An agent asks:

> What can be done from here?

Thus:

```text
OBSERVATIONAL ACCESS
≠
AGENTIVE ACCESS
```

# 16. Observer/Agent Firewall

Freeze the comparator-level firewall:

> **Observation ≠ agency.**

More explicitly:

```text
can observe X
≠
can predict X
≠
can causally affect X
≠
is authorized to alter X
```

This is highly compatible with FSP’s authorization architecture.

# 18. Price and Ismael on Causation

Price emphasizes the pragmatic and perspectival role of causal vocabulary.

Ismael refines this by distinguishing ways in which causal concepts may depend on agents.

The joint lesson for FSP is:

> **Agent-relative causal salience need not imply arbitrary subjectivism.**

The structure may be objective while its practical significance depends on the agent’s location and intervention possibilities.

---

# 19. Pragmatic Dependence

One important Ismaelian distinction is **pragmatic dependence**.

A structure may be objectively present while the reason it matters as causal, salient, or explanatory depends on the needs and capacities of agents.

For FSP:

```text
OBJECTIVE STRUCTURE
≠
AGENT-RELATIVE SIGNIFICANCE
```

But:

> **Agent-relative significance ≠ mere projection.**

---

# 20. Three Forms of Perspective Dependence

FSP should avoid using “perspectival” without qualification.

At least distinguish:

## Pragmatic dependence

The structure is objective; its practical role depends on agents.

## Implicit relativization

Truth conditions depend on contextual parameters.

## Indexicality

The expression contains context-sensitive reference.

This distinction can prevent overgeneralized perspectivalism.

# 28. Price and Ismael on Return

FSP Return currently means consequential re-entry into lived Doing.

Ismael suggests a refinement:

> **Return includes re-location.**

After inquiry or intervention, the agent must determine:

- where am I now?
- what has changed?
- what can I access?
- what can I do?
- what constraints now apply?
- what future possibilities have opened or closed?

Thus:

```text
RETURN
=

+ revised map
+ revised self-location
+ revised capability
+ REOPEN conditions
```

when self-location matters.

# 33. Selective Usable Articulation

> **A map is a selectively usable articulation of relations relevant to inquiry or action.**

The central methodological rule becomes:

> **Before asking what a map represents, ask what a situated agent is doing with it. Before asking what follows from it, ask where that agent stands and what changes when the map is carried elsewhere.**
