# Agency Under Constraint

## Free Will, Responsibility, Neuroethics, Transformation, and the Situated Human Agent

**Status:** SOURCE-REVIEWED RESEARCH ORIENTATION — NOT PUBLIC-READY  
**Research region:** Situated Agency and Transformation; Mind, Perception, and Phenomenology; Institutions, Power, and Ethical Coordination.

Human beings encounter themselves as agents: we deliberate, form intentions, resist impulses, make commitments, and answer for at least some of what we do. Yet contemporary inquiry makes agency harder to treat as an isolated power of choice. Human action depends on brains and bodies, learned habits, social relationships, institutions, resources, information, technologies, and histories. The philosophical problem is therefore no longer exhausted by asking whether an action was free or determined. A broader research question is required: **what kind of agency exists here, what enables or constrains it, and what follows ethically from the answer?**

The orientation begins from several existing maps rather than treating one as complete. Simon's bounded-rationality programme shows why finite agents cannot be modeled as unlimited optimizers. Ismael's work on the situated self and agentive perspective helps distinguish public description from the position of an embedded agent. Neuroscience supplies increasingly detailed causal and functional accounts of capacities relevant to behavior. Law and ethics ask different questions about responsibility, competence, blame, treatment, protection, and authorization.

FSP enters where these descriptions cross.

The historical Phineas Gage literature is useful precisely because the familiar textbook story is itself a reconstruction assembled from injury reports, behavioral testimony, later medical interpretation, and historical retelling. The research lesson is not simply that frontal injury "changes personality." It is that a neurological event may alter capacities, relationships, employment, self-regulation, and future reachability while the historical dossier remains distinct from the person.

The Burns–Swerdlow orbitofrontal-tumor case makes the bridge problem sharper. The reported behavioral change, tumor removal, behavioral improvement, recurrence, and subsequent treatment provide unusually important causal evidence. But even strong causal evidence does not make the ethical conclusion automatic. The bridge still requires questions about which capacities were impaired, what control remained, what risks were present, what forms of responsibility are being assessed, and which medical, legal, or ethical authorities may act.

FSP therefore represents the chain as:

**CAUSAL EXPLANATION → CAPABILITY / CONTROL → RESPONSIBILITY JUDGMENT → AUTHORIZATION → INTERVENTION → RETURN**

Each arrow carries a burden.

This is why **causal explanation ≠ ethical verdict** and **impairment ≠ complete absence of agency**.

The same architecture applies beyond neurological impairment. Poverty, coercion, addiction, institutional incentives, misinformation, algorithmic recommendation, trauma, sleep, education, social support, and AI assistance can all alter a person's practical field without reducing the person to one cause. FSP's Transformation Architecture makes the distinctions explicit:

**possible ≠ feasible ≠ visible ≠ selectable ≠ authorized ≠ enacted.**

The free-will debate remains philosophically important, but its answers are target-sensitive. A metaphysical inquiry about alternative possibilities is not identical with a physician's question about decision capacity, a court's question about culpability, a teacher's question about support, or a friend's question about forgiveness. FSP calls this target binding: know which question your answer is answering.

Contemporary AI extends the problem from individual agency to distributed agency. A decision may involve data, a model, a generated output, a professional judgment, organizational rules, and an authorized action. Distributed causation does not eliminate responsibility; it makes the responsibility structure something that must be mapped.

The research orientation therefore uses the Four-Move governance surface:

**PORTABILITY:** what travels from causal or statistical evidence into an agential claim?

**CEILING:** how far does that evidence justify conclusions about control or responsibility?

**AUTHORIZATION:** who may diagnose, restrain, punish, treat, recommend, or decide?

**RETURN:** what happens after intervention, and what should reopen?

**REMAINDER:** what remains unknown about motive, experience, capacity, or consequence?

### Claim ceiling

This orientation does not establish that determinism is true or false, that neuroscience eliminates free will, that impairment automatically excuses conduct, or that one model of responsibility is correct. It proposes a governed research environment for distinguishing causal explanation, capability, responsibility, and authorized response.

### Research frontier

The main open questions concern how dimensions of capability map onto responsibility, how distributed AI-mediated decisions should allocate responsibility, how first-person reports constrain public models, and how institutions can repair or enlarge agency without replacing accountability.
