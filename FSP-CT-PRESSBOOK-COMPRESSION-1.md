# PART I — HISTORY OF ETHICS PRESSBOOK INTRODUCTION

# Thinking Ethically as a Situated Human Inquirer

Ethics does not begin when someone opens a philosophy book.

Long before we encounter Socrates, Aristotle, Aquinas, Hume, Kant, Mill, Marx, Nietzsche, Rawls, or contemporary approaches to care and capability, we are already trying to find our way through situations involving other people, expectations, rules, uncertainty, consequences, loyalties, disagreements, institutions, and unequal power.

We notice some things and miss others. We interpret what people meant. We decide which facts matter. We inherit habits and expectations. We make judgments before we know everything. We act, encounter consequences, and sometimes revise what we thought.

In that broad sense, ordinary human life already has the structure of inquiry.

This course asks what happens when we begin to examine that inquiry deliberately.

Ethics therefore involves more than asking, “What should I do?” It also asks what situation we are actually responding to, what reasons support our judgment, how our habits and expectations were formed, what was really within someone’s control, why a rule or authority should bind, whose perspective is represented, who benefits or bears burdens, and what would make us revise our present view.

The historical philosophers in this book will not all answer those questions in the same way. That disagreement is part of the course.

The purpose of the critical-thinking framework is not to make them agree. It is to help us see more clearly **what kind of ethical work a philosopher, argument, or case is asking us to do.**

---

## 1. You Are Already an Inquirer

No one encounters an ethical situation from nowhere.

We enter situations with limited attention, prior experience, emotions, concepts, social roles, relationships, and histories. We may hear one person’s account before another’s. A rule may already define the problem before we have asked whether that framing is adequate.

For this course, we will call any selective articulation of a situation a **map**.

A map can be a description, story, category, rule, statistic, memory, institutional record, or interpretation. Maps can be very useful. But they remain maps.

The first recurring firewall is:

> **Map ≠ World ≠ Person.**

A description of another person is not that person. A diagnosis is not the whole person. A manager’s account of an employee is not the employee. A moral theory is not the entire situation to which it is applied.

So our first ethical habit is simple:

> **Before reasoning confidently from a situation, inspect the map.**

Ask:

**What happened? What am I adding? What might I be missing?**

The way a situation appears to us also has a history. Families, communities, languages, institutions, professions, technologies, rewards, failures, and relationships shape what becomes easy or difficult to notice.

We will call that reciprocal process an **assembly history**.

But assembly history does not settle the ethical question.

> **Formation ≠ justification.**

and:

> **Origin ≠ validity.**

Understanding how a value or judgment developed can deepen inquiry without proving that the value is right or wrong.

---

## 2. Critical Thinking Tests Reasons

Familiar critical-thinking skills remain essential in this course.

We will reconstruct arguments, distinguish premises from conclusions, examine deduction and induction, compare explanations, use analogy, evaluate evidence, inspect causal claims, identify fallacies, consider objections, and revise conclusions.

FSP does not replace those skills.

It extends them.

Why is an extension useful?

Because a person can reason very carefully from an incomplete map.

A strong argument can support a judgment without settling who may act on that judgment.

A policy can produce benefits while distributing serious burdens unfairly.

An institution can enforce a rule without making that rule morally legitimate.

So this course will ask us to inspect not only arguments, but also the transitions around them.

---

## 3. Governed Ethical Inquiry

A useful way to picture ethical inquiry is:

```text
lived situation
↔ partial map
↔ reasons and evidence
↔ bounded judgment
↔ standing / authorization
↔ action
↔ consequences
↔ REOPEN
```

This is not a formula that every case follows step by step. Real inquiry loops, overlaps, and doubles back.

The point is diagnostic.

Sometimes the main problem is an incomplete representation.

Sometimes it is a weak reason.

Sometimes we confuse power with moral authority.

Sometimes we ignore constraint when assigning responsibility.

Sometimes a beneficial intervention bypasses another person’s agency.

Sometimes a conclusion is reasonable but the proposed intervention is not yet authorized.

This is what this book means by **governed critical thinking**.

Across the course you will gradually acquire a larger Tool Rack for these different problems.

But one rule applies from the beginning:

> **Tool ≠ Verdict.**

A Tool helps us inspect the inquiry. It does not decide the case for us.

---

## 4. Six Questions You Can Use Now

When a difficult ethical case appears, begin with six questions.

### 1. What is my map?

What happened? What am I adding? What or who might be missing?

### 2. What are the reasons?

What supports the judgment? What actually follows from those reasons?

### 3. What shaped the situation?

What habits, constraints, institutions, histories, or power relations matter?

### 4. Who is affected and who has standing?

Whose perspective is represented? Who may decide, recommend, constrain, or intervene?

### 5. What is my claim ceiling?

What does not yet follow from the evidence or reasoning I have?

### 6. What would REOPEN this?

What new evidence, consequence, challenge, changed condition, or newly visible person would make me reconsider?

That routine is enough to begin.

The chapters will deepen it.

---

## 5. Judgment Is Not the Same as Authorization

Suppose the evidence supports a conclusion.

Another question remains:

> **What does that conclusion authorize anyone to do?**

A physician may have good evidence but still need consent.

A manager may identify a real problem but lack moral standing to impose one particular solution.

A government may predict a benefit while still needing to justify coercion.

A teacher may reasonably judge that a student needs help while still needing to preserve the student’s agency.

So this course will repeatedly distinguish:

> **Supported judgment ≠ unrestricted authorization.**

and:

> **Possibility ≠ Recommendation ≠ Authorization.**

Generating an option does not recommend it.

Recommending an option does not establish who may implement it.

Ethical reasoning therefore extends beyond belief into standing, legitimacy, responsibility, and action.

This becomes especially important when individual judgments become institutional rules, procedures, policies, incentives, or professional practices.

---

## 6. Claim Ceilings, REOPEN, and the History Ahead

One of the simplest habits in this course is:

> **Ask what does not yet follow.**

A useful insight can become an overclaim if we carry it too far.

Examples include:

> **Formation ≠ justification.**

> **Power ≠ legitimacy.**

> **Benefit ≠ complete justification.**

> **Current judgment ≠ final closure.**

A **claim ceiling** does not weaken inquiry. It makes the conclusion more precise.

**REOPEN** asks what would return a present judgment to inquiry.

New evidence? Unexpected harm? A person left out? A failed prediction? A challenge to authority? Changed circumstances? A better explanation?

REOPEN does not mean endless hesitation. It means that a judgment can guide action without pretending to be beyond correction.

The framework also does not make the final judgment for you. Philosophers, arguments, cases, instructors, simulations, and AI tools may help you inspect the problem, but your task is still to make and defend a bounded judgment in your own words.

As the course develops, the historical philosophers will enlarge what ethical inquiry can notice.

Early chapters will sharpen attention, reason-giving, formation, and agency.

Middle chapters will make authority, legitimacy, perspective, respect, consequence, and liberty more visible.

Later chapters will deepen our understanding of power, fairness, capability, dependence, and cooperation.

Real cases will pressure-test those gains.

The history of ethics can therefore be read not only as a sequence of competing theories, but also as a cumulative repertoire of ways human beings have learned to inspect moral situations more carefully.

The governing question for the course is not only:

> **Which philosopher is right?**

It is also:

> **What does this philosopher help us see that we might otherwise miss, what follows from seeing it, what does not yet follow, and how should that insight change the way we inquire and act?**

That question carries the history of ethics back into lived Doing.

---

# PART II — HISTORY OF INQUIRY PRESSBOOK INTRODUCTION

# Inquiry Begins Before Science Has a Name

Human beings were inquirers before there were laboratories, scientific journals, universities, or formal theories of method.

We notice patterns. We compare cases. We remember what happened before. We anticipate what might happen next. We use tools. We coordinate with others. We make distinctions, build explanations, test expectations against resistance, and revise what we thought we knew.

Over time, some of these practices become more explicit, more disciplined, more public, more instrumented, and more institutionally organized.

The history of inquiry is therefore not simply the history of correct theories replacing incorrect ones.

It is also the history of changing ways to observe, represent, compare, measure, explain, argue, test, model, authorize, revise, and share knowledge.

This course asks us to follow those changes.

It also asks us to notice that inquiry is always conducted by situated human beings using limited instruments, inherited concepts, social institutions, learned practices, and partial evidence.

That means critical thinking in the history of inquiry requires more than checking whether an argument is valid.

We also need to ask what was actually observed, how it was represented, what an instrument made visible, what evidence licensed, which alternatives remained open, what background assumptions mattered, and what would force revision.

---

## 1. You Are Already an Inquirer

Scientific inquiry often begins with observation, but observation is never simply “the world entering the mind unchanged.”

What can be observed depends partly on bodily capacities, instruments, learned distinctions, concepts, procedures, and recording practices.

A telescope, microscope, clock, interferometer, spectrograph, simulation, or statistical model makes some features available for inquiry and not others.

So the first recurring firewall is:

> **Map ≠ World.**

A map can be a diagram, measurement, data table, coordinate system, model, theory, reconstruction, simulation, or verbal description.

Maps are indispensable.

But they are not identical with what they represent.

This gives us an important inquiry habit:

> **Before asking whether a conclusion is correct, ask what representation made the conclusion possible.**

Scientific inquiry also has an assembly history.

Investigators inherit concepts, instruments, mathematical techniques, laboratories, standards, textbooks, accepted constants, and expectations about what counts as a problem or explanation.

Those inheritances make inquiry possible, but they can also make alternatives harder to see.

So we preserve another firewall:

> **Origin ≠ validity.**

Understanding how a method, model, or concept developed does not by itself tell us whether it is true or adequate now.

---

## 2. Critical Thinking Tests Reasons and Evidence

Familiar critical-thinking skills remain central here too.

We will use deduction, induction, abduction, analogy, causal reasoning, source evaluation, probability, uncertainty, counterexamples, and argument reconstruction.

FSP does not replace those skills.

It extends them.

Why is an extension useful?

Because strong reasoning can still begin from a poor representation.

A measurement can be precise while its interpretation remains uncertain.

A successful model can organize evidence without settling ontology.

A scientific community can have good reasons for accepting a result while leaving deeper questions open.

So the course will ask us to inspect not only arguments, but also how observation, representation, evidence, model choice, institutional validation, and revision interact.

---

## 3. Governed Inquiry

A useful way to picture inquiry is:

```text
encounter / observation
↔ representation
↔ question
↔ model or explanation
↔ evidence
↔ bounded judgment
↔ institutional validation
↔ anomaly / resistance
↔ revision / REOPEN
```

This is not a universal scientific method.

Real inquiry is messier.

Observation can change a model.

A model can change what investigators look for.

New instruments can create new questions.

Evidence can constrain inquiry without uniquely identifying which assumption should be revised.

A reliable result can still leave interpretation open.

The point is diagnostic.

> **What kind of inquiry problem are we facing now?**

One recurring rule applies throughout:

> **Tool ≠ Verdict.**

A method, model, or analytic Tool can expose a dependency, constrain inference, or improve the representation without automatically settling every question.

---

## 4. Six Questions You Can Use Now

When you encounter a historical scientific case, begin with six questions.

### 1. What was observed or made measurable?

What entered the inquiry, through which instrument, procedure, or representation?

### 2. What inference was licensed?

What conclusion was supported, and what did not yet follow?

### 3. What alternatives remained?

What rival explanation, model, or auxiliary assumption was still live?

### 4. What shaped the inquiry?

Which concepts, instruments, institutions, practices, or background commitments mattered?

### 5. What is the claim ceiling?

How far can the evidence responsibly carry us?

### 6. What would REOPEN the case?

What anomaly, new evidence, failed prediction, conceptual change, or better explanation would matter?

That routine is enough to begin.

The historical episodes will make it more precise.

---

## 5. Models, Instruments, and Sources Need Claim Ceilings

Instruments can extend what humans detect, measure, compare, and record.

But an instrument reading still has to be calibrated, interpreted, and connected to a claim.

So:

> **Instrument reading ≠ uninterpreted fact.**

Likewise, models can be extraordinarily successful without becoming identical with reality.

So:

> **Model success ≠ ontology.**

A model may predict well, organize evidence, unify phenomena, or guide successful intervention.

Those achievements matter.

But they do not automatically establish that the model’s structure is the final description of the world.

Historical inquiry also has a source problem.

A primary historical source is not the same thing as a modern scholarly reconstruction.

A modern scholarly reconstruction is not the same thing as the historical actor’s own understanding.

An FSP case encoding is not evidence that historical investigators themselves thought in FSP categories.

So we preserve:

> **Historical source ≠ scholarly reconstruction ≠ contemporary scientific source ≠ FSP reconstruction.**

These distinctions are all forms of claim-ceiling discipline.

---

## 6. REOPEN and the History Ahead

One of the simplest habits in this course is:

> **Ask what does not yet follow.**

Examples include:

> **Map ≠ World.**

> **Evidence ≠ stronger conclusion than it supports.**

> **Model success ≠ ontology.**

> **Authority ≠ truth.**

> **Current judgment ≠ final closure.**

A claim ceiling does not weaken inquiry. It protects precision.

**REOPEN** asks what would return a present settlement to inquiry.

A failed prediction? A new instrument? An anomaly? A better measurement? A rival model with greater explanatory success? A replication failure? A new primary source? A changed estimate of uncertainty?

REOPEN does not mean every result is perpetually doubtful.

It means confidence remains connected to conditions of warrant.

The framework also does not make the final judgment for you. It helps you inspect the inquiry, but you still have to explain what you think the evidence supports and what remains unsettled.

As the course develops, historical episodes will enlarge what inquiry can observe, represent, test, explain, compare, and revise.

The course will move through cases ranging from Galileo and Foucault to Neptune/Vulcan, relativity, Kuhn, thought experiments, and contemporary cosmology.

Those cases will not all instantiate one method.

Instead, each will reveal a different pressure point in inquiry.

The governing question for the course is not only:

> **Which theory won?**

It is also:

> **What changed in what investigators could observe, represent, infer, test, explain, and revise—and what does that history teach us about how inquiry should be governed now?**

That question carries the history of inquiry back into present Doing.
