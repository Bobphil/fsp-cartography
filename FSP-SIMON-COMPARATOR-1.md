# FSP-SIMON-COMPARATOR-1

## Near-Decomposability, Hierarchy, Bounded Rationality, Design, Search, Artificial Systems, Relation–Transition–Return, and the Governance Gap

**Status:** COMPARATOR / PHILOSOPHICAL CLARIFICATION — VERSION 1  
**Parent:** FSP-CONSTITUTIONAL-COMPRESSION-1; FSP-CONSTITUTIONAL-REGRESSION-1  
**Comparator:** Herbert A. Simon — bounded rationality, satisficing, problem solving, design, artificial systems, hierarchy, and near-decomposability  
**Primary purpose:** Identify structural affinities and decisive differences between Simon’s sciences of the artificial and FSP’s constitutional grammar, without collapsing one into the other or claiming intellectual derivation where only structural convergence is established.  
**Primary claim ceiling:** This document does not claim that Simon anticipated FSP, that FSP supersedes Simon, that FSP is a theory of bounded rationality, or that Simon’s framework lacks normative resources altogether. It isolates a specific comparative result: Simon provides a deep architecture of bounded adaptation, design, search, and decomposition; FSP adds an explicit governance layer for transitions among partial maps, scales, roles, representations, judgments, authorizations, and consequences.

# 1. Why Simon Is the Right Comparator Now

The Simon comparison becomes especially valuable only after constitutional compression.

Before compression, FSP contained too many object families to know what was actually being compared.

After **FSP-CONSTITUTIONAL-COMPRESSION-1** and **FSP-CONSTITUTIONAL-REGRESSION-1**, the portable FSP claim is much smaller:

```text
PARTIAL MAP
→ RELATION
→ GOVERNED TRANSITION
→ CLAIM CEILING / REMAINDER
→ AUTHORIZATION
→ CONSEQUENCE
→ REOPEN
→ RETURN
```

Simon’s work repeatedly sought a similarly portable level of description across:

- organizations;
- economics;
- human problem solving;
- computation;
- artificial intelligence;
- engineering design;
- planning;
- hierarchy;
- complex systems.

The comparison is therefore not superficial.

Both programs seek structures that survive changes in domain.

But they do not seek the same structures for the same purpose.

---

# 2. Simon’s Core Problem

Simon’s central problem can be stated as:

> **How can finite agents and artificial systems achieve workable adaptation in environments too complex for exhaustive optimization?**

This question generates several major Simonian commitments:

1. rationality is bounded by information and computation;
2. agents often satisfice rather than optimize;
3. search is selective rather than exhaustive;
4. problem structure and environmental structure matter;
5. artificial systems can be understood as interfaces between inner and outer environments;
6. complex systems often exhibit hierarchy;
7. near-decomposability makes complex systems tractable;
8. design concerns changing existing situations into preferred ones;
9. procedural rationality matters when global optimization is infeasible.

FSP shares much of the pressure behind these commitments.

But FSP asks a further family of questions Simon does not make constitutional:

> **What licenses the transition? What is preserved? What is lost? What remains unresolved? Who may act? What consequences follow? What must be reopened?**

This is the initial statement of the **governance gap**.

---

# 3. Bounded Rationality

Simon’s bounded rationality is not simply the claim that people are irrational.

It is the claim that realistic rational behavior must be modeled under:

- limited information;
- limited computational capacity;
- limited time;
- limited search;
- finite planning horizons;
- structured environments.

A classic Simonian shift is:

```text
global optimization
↓
procedural rationality
↓
satisficing / heuristic search
```

This has a strong affinity with FSP.

FSP also rejects architectures that silently assume:

- complete information;
- complete maps;
- exhaustive search;
- one universal perspective;
- final closure.

The overlap can be written:

```text
SIMON:
finite agent + structured environment
→ bounded search
→ satisfactory action

FSP:
partial map + bounded relation access
→ governed transition
→ bounded judgment / action
```

The similarity is real.

The difference is also real.

Simon asks primarily:

> How can a bounded agent find a workable course of action?

FSP asks additionally:

> What does the available evidence license the agent to claim, recommend, authorize, impose, or leave unresolved?

---

# 4. Satisficing and FSP

Satisficing is one of Simon’s most portable ideas.

A satisficing agent searches until it finds an option that meets an aspiration level rather than proving global optimality.

This maps naturally onto several FSP commitments:

- partial maps;
- bounded judgment;
- no requirement of exhaustive search;
- action under uncertainty;
- claim ceilings;
- REOPEN.

But the concepts are not identical.

Satisficing answers:

> **When may search stop for practical choice?**

A claim ceiling answers:

> **How strong may the resulting claim become?**

REOPEN answers:

> **Under what conditions must the settlement become revisable?**

Authorization asks:

> **Even if this option is satisfactory, who may choose or impose it?**

Therefore:

> **Satisficing ≠ bounded judgment ≠ authorization ≠ closure.**

This is one of the most important comparator firewalls.

---

# 5. Aspiration Levels and Claim Ceilings

There is a tempting but misleading analogy:

```text
Simon aspiration level
≈
FSP claim ceiling
```

This should be rejected.

An **aspiration level** is a threshold for acceptable performance or choice.

A **claim ceiling** limits the inferential strength of a conclusion.

They govern different dimensions.

Example:

A medical intervention may satisfice relative to a treatment objective.

That does not imply:

- the causal evidence is strong;
- the mechanism is known;
- the patient has consented;
- the clinician has authority to proceed;
- long-term harms are bounded;
- alternative treatments have been exhausted.

Thus:

> **Performance sufficiency ≠ epistemic sufficiency.**

and:

> **Epistemic sufficiency ≠ authorization.**

FSP therefore adds governance around the stopping rules that bounded rationality makes necessary.

---

# 6. Search

Simon’s work on problem solving and AI treats search as selective traversal through a structured problem space.

This insight strongly anticipates the pressure behind FSP’s Transformation Architecture:

```text
current state
→ candidate moves
→ reachable states
→ evaluation
→ selected path
```

FSP already distinguishes:

- possible;
- feasible;
- visible;
- selectable;
- authorized;
- implemented.

This decomposition shows where Simon and FSP part company.

Simonian search focuses primarily on the relation among:

- problem representation;
- candidate moves;
- heuristic selectivity;
- computational feasibility;
- goal attainment.

FSP adds:

- who constructed the problem space;
- which possibilities are invisible to the current PRF;
- which moves are feasible but unauthorized;
- which actions alter future capability;
- which paths create lock-in;
- which consequences affect others;
- which remainders must survive search termination.

Thus:

> **Search space ≠ morally or institutionally permissible action space.**

and:

> **Reachable ≠ selectable ≠ authorized.**

---

# 7. Search Space Construction

A crucial Simonian insight is that search is made tractable by representation.

The problem is not merely traversed.

It is represented in a way that exposes useful structure.

FSP strongly agrees.

But FSP’s map firewall adds:

> **Problem representation ≠ problem itself.**

This creates a distinct governance question:

> Which possibilities disappeared when the problem was represented this way?

A search algorithm can be impeccable relative to its problem representation while the representation itself omits:

- affected people;
- alternative goals;
- institutional constraints;
- moral standing;
- long-term consequence;
- scale mismatch;
- uncertainty;
- translation loss.

This is a major FSP extension of the Simonian problem-space framework.

---

# 8. Heuristics

Simon treats heuristics as necessary mechanisms of intelligent selectivity.

FSP has no reason to reject this.

But FSP places heuristics under the partial-map architecture.

A heuristic may be:

- efficient;
- empirically successful;
- computationally necessary;
- locally robust;

while still carrying hidden transition burdens.

Therefore:

> **Heuristic success ≠ unrestricted inferential license.**

Examples:

- triage heuristic;
- institutional risk score;
- diagnostic shortcut;
- scientific model selection rule;
- classroom rubric;
- search-ranking algorithm.

FSP asks what the heuristic preserves, what it suppresses, and what happens when its output is carried into action.

---

# 9. The Artifact as Interface

One of Simon’s most important formulations is that an artificial system can be understood through the relation among:

```text
goal / purpose
↕
artifact
↕
environment
```

The artifact functions as an interface between an inner environment and an outer environment.

This is one of the strongest structural affinities with FSP.

FSP likewise resists analyzing a system in isolation.

It asks about relations among:

- bearer;
- environment;
- task;
- constraints;
- representation;
- institution;
- capability;
- consequence.

The shared insight is:

> **Behavior cannot be understood solely from internal structure.**

But FSP adds a further question:

> Which relations are being preserved when the artifact is interpreted as an interface, and what transitions become licensed by that interpretation?

---

# 10. Inner and Outer Environments

Simon’s inner–outer distinction offers a powerful explanation of adaptation.

An artifact’s observable behavior may reflect the structure of the external environment more strongly than the details of internal realization.

This supports FSP’s anti-privilege stance.

A functional pattern may be multiply realized.

However:

> **Functional equivalence ≠ ontological identity.**

and:

> **Common interface role ≠ common phenomenology, agency, standing, or authorization.**

This is especially important for:

- AI;
- synthetic biology;
- animal cognition;
- institutions;
- hybrid systems.

FSP therefore accepts the portability of role while governing the temptation to infer sameness of realization.

---

# 11. Artificial Systems and Ontological Non-Privilege

Simon’s sciences of the artificial encourage inquiry into systems in terms of function, adaptation, and design rather than only material composition.

FSP shares this pluralism.

But FSP makes a different constitutional move:

> **No representation, scale, ontology, or agent-native partition receives universal privilege merely because it is locally successful.**

This is not present in Simon in the same form.

Simon’s framework permits abstraction from implementation where the task permits it.

FSP adds:

> **Task-local abstraction ≠ universal ontological privilege.**

This is especially important when functional description travels into:

- consciousness claims;
- moral standing;
- legal personhood;
- medical intervention;
- institutional authority.

---

# 12. Design

Simon’s science of design is one of the clearest bridges to FSP.

Design concerns the transformation of existing situations into preferred ones.

The Simonian design arc can be compressed:

```text
current state
→ goal / preferred state
→ search
→ design
→ implementation
```

FSP’s Transformation Architecture closely overlaps.

But FSP decomposes the path further:

```text
current state
→ visible possibilities
→ feasible possibilities
→ selectable possibilities
→ authorized possibilities
→ implemented transition
→ consequence
→ repair / REOPEN
→ Return
```

This reveals the governance gap.

Simon’s design science asks how preferred states can be reached.

FSP asks additionally:

- preferred by whom?
- under which normative profile?
- who has standing?
- who bears the burden?
- who may authorize the transformation?
- what capabilities are expanded?
- what capabilities are enclosed?
- what remainder persists?
- what happens after implementation?

---

# 13. Preferred States

A design framework needs some criterion of preference.

Simon was fully aware that design introduces normative questions.

FSP’s distinctive move is to prevent the criterion from disappearing into the formalism.

FSP therefore requires separation among:

```text
functional success
normative evaluation
authorization
consequence
```

An institution can be efficiently redesigned for a goal that should not govern.

A treatment can achieve a physiological target while violating consent.

An optimization system can improve a metric while enclosing capability.

A policy can satisfy its stated objective while transferring costs to people who lacked standing in the design process.

Therefore:

> **Preferred-state reachability ≠ legitimacy.**

This is a central FSP addition.

---

# 14. Design and Standing

Simon’s framework gives us strong tools for analyzing:

- goals;
- constraints;
- means;
- search;
- adaptation;
- implementation.

FSP adds a question that must remain separate:

> **Who counts in defining the design problem?**

This is the standing problem.

A system can be well-designed relative to one actor’s goals while being destructive relative to another’s capabilities.

Therefore:

> **Design success for stakeholder A ≠ justified intervention on stakeholder B.**

The Normative Profile architecture exists precisely because design cannot settle standing by itself.

---

# 15. Near-Decomposability

Near-decomposability is perhaps the most important Simonian comparator for constitutional compression.

The basic insight is that many complex systems exhibit:

- relatively strong interaction within subsystems;
- weaker but nonzero interaction among subsystems;
- characteristic time-scale separation;
- tractable approximations based on partial decomposition.

This provides a deep explanation of why complex systems can often be analyzed without representing every interaction at full detail.

FSP gains an important comparator claim:

> **Constitutional compression is plausible because many inquiry situations are locally decomposable enough to permit selective representation without requiring total reconstruction.**

But FSP must add a firewall:

> **Near-decomposability ≠ safe independence.**

Weak coupling can still matter decisively when:

- consequences accumulate;
- institutional effects amplify;
- threshold crossings occur;
- delayed feedback appears;
- rare events dominate;
- standing changes;
- cross-scale bridges are high burden.

---

# 16. Near-Decomposability and Partial Maps

Simon helps explain why partial maps can work.

If a system is sufficiently near-decomposable, local structure can be analyzed while abstracting from many weak interactions.

FSP adds:

> **A useful local map must preserve the relations needed for the current claim.**

Therefore the two frameworks align:

```text
SIMON:
near-decomposable structure
→ tractable local description

FSP:
partial map
→ task-relative relation preservation
```

The FSP addition is explicit governance over when omitted couplings become relevant.

---

# 17. Decomposition and Bridge Burden

Near-decomposability creates a natural FSP question.

If subsystem A and subsystem B are analyzed separately:

> What burden appears when a claim crosses between them?

This yields:

```text
decomposition
↓
local model
↓
cross-subsystem transition
↓
bridge burden
```

Thus Simon helps explain **why decomposition is useful**.

FSP explains **how to govern recomposition**.

This may be the deepest architectural relation between the two frameworks.

---

# 18. Hierarchy

Simon treats hierarchy broadly as systems decomposable into successive sets of subsystems.

FSP should accept hierarchy as one possible structural organization without turning it into a universal metaphysics.

The constitutional regression already established:

> **ScaleKind ≠ ontology.**

The Simon comparator adds:

> **Hierarchy ≠ universal structure of reality.**

Hierarchy may be:

- physical;
- organizational;
- representational;
- institutional;
- temporal;
- functional.

FSP requires the scale kind and relation domain to remain visible when ambiguity matters.

---

# 19. Hierarchy and FSP Scale Architecture

Simon’s hierarchy work directly supports the motivation behind:

- ScaleKind;
- ScaleRelation;
- RelationDomain;
- ScaleBridgeRecord;
- BridgeBurden.

But Simon does not justify promoting all of these FSP objects into Core.

Instead, Simon supports the weaker claim:

> **Cross-scale inquiry is often tractable because systems can be selectively partitioned, but the validity of cross-scale inference depends on the relations preserved by the partition.**

This is exactly the level identified by constitutional compression.

---

# 20. Near-Decomposability and Emergence

The Simon comparator strengthens the decision to demote `EmergenceRecord`.

Simon can explain much complex organization using:

- hierarchy;
- coupling;
- time-scale separation;
- subsystem interaction;

without requiring emergence language at every level.

Therefore:

> **Complex organization ≠ automatic emergence claim.**

FSP should continue to use emergence only when a distinct emergence thesis is actually under investigation.

# 21. Bounded Rationality and Partial Maps

Simon’s bounded agent never possesses the whole relevant state space.

FSP’s partial map makes a similar but more general claim.

The two can be aligned:

```text
bounded information
→ partial map

bounded computation
→ selective processing

bounded search
→ satisficing / heuristic choice

bounded evidence
→ claim ceiling

bounded translation
→ remainder
```

But only the first three are characteristically Simonian.

The latter two are central FSP governance additions.

---

# 22. Bounded Rationality and REOPEN

Satisficing naturally creates revisability.

A satisfactory solution today may become unsatisfactory when:

- aspiration levels change;
- new information arrives;
- the environment changes;
- the system learns;
- constraints shift.

FSP formalizes this with REOPEN.

But FSP’s REOPEN is stronger than adaptive search alone.

It also activates when:

- standing changes;
- authorization fails;
- harms emerge;
- the target was misbound;
- translation loss becomes decision-relevant;
- institutional amplification produces unexpected consequences.

Thus:

> **Adaptive revision ≠ full governance REOPEN.**

---

# 23. Problem Solving and Inquiry

Simon’s problem-solving architecture often assumes a task with enough structure to support search.

FSP must also handle cases where the task itself is unstable.

Examples:

- the wrong question was asked;
- the target changed;
- categories shifted historically;
- the relevant scale was misidentified;
- another stakeholder’s perspective changes what counts as the problem;
- a scientific anomaly redefines the hypothesis space.

This gives a strong distinction:

> **Solving within a problem representation ≠ governing the formation of the problem representation.**

FSP places map formation, PRF, assembly history, perspective, and target binding upstream of search.

---

# 24. Search-Space Expansion

Simon’s problem solving often concerns search within a structured space.

FSP Transformation Architecture also allows:

> the hypothesis or possibility space itself can change.

This can occur through:

- new evidence;
- new instruments;
- new collaborators;
- new categories;
- new scales;
- altered capabilities;
- new institutional arrangements.

Therefore:

> **Search-space traversal ≠ search-space generation.**

This is not a rejection of Simon.

It is a frontier extension of the same bounded-search problem.

# 25. Artificial Intelligence

Simon was foundational to AI as the study of intelligent problem solving through representation, search, heuristics, and symbolic processes.

FSP should not present itself as an alternative AI theory.

Its relation is different.

FSP asks how AI participation changes:

- access;
- evidence;
- translation;
- capability;
- authority;
- visibility;
- decision consequence;
- Return.

Thus:

> **AI competence ≠ epistemic authority.**

> **AI contribution ≠ standing.**

> **AI recommendation ≠ authorization.**

These are FSP governance questions around artificial participation.

# 26. Computational Prosthesis

FSP’s concept of cognitive or computational prosthesis is especially compatible with Simon.

A tool can expand:

- search;
- memory;
- comparison;
- simulation;
- pattern detection;
- possibility generation.

But FSP imposes a return requirement.

The computational system may assist inquiry.

The human or institution must still govern:

- claim strength;
- standing;
- authorization;
- consequence;
- accountability;
- REOPEN.

This is a crucial extension from problem-solving support to governed Doing.

- information access;

# 28. Organizational Near-Decomposability

Organizations often exhibit local clustering:

- departments;
- teams;
- roles;
- reporting lines;
- workflows;
- information channels.

Near-decomposability helps explain why local routines can function.

FSP asks when local decomposition fails.

Examples:

- safety information trapped in one unit;
- distributed harms invisible to a central metric;
- local optimization damaging system capability;
- authority structure suppressing dissent;
- cross-unit evidence losing provenance.

Thus:

> **Organizational decomposability ≠ governance sufficiency.**

---

# 29. Institutional Amplification

Simon analyzes how organizational structure shapes behavior.

FSP adds the concept of institutional amplification.

A local judgment can become consequential when embedded in:

- policy;
- workflow;
- scoring system;
- classification system;
- automated procedure;
- authority chain.

The FSP question is not merely:

> How does the organization process information?

It is also:

> What happens when a partial map becomes institutionally executable?

This is a major part of the governance gap.

---

# 30. Procedural Rationality

Simon’s procedural rationality is highly compatible with FSP.

Both emphasize the path by which a conclusion or choice is reached rather than only the final output.

FSP’s contribution is to widen procedure into governance.

A decision procedure can be computationally reasonable while still failing:

- provenance;
- standing;
- authorization;
- fairness;
- repair;
- claim ceiling;
- REOPEN.

Thus:

> **Procedural rationality ≠ complete governance adequacy.**

# 31. Simonian Compression

Simon repeatedly sought ways to make complexity tractable through:

- abstraction;
- hierarchy;
- decomposition;
- selective search;
- representation;
- procedural approximation.

This is a genuine precursor in spirit to FSP constitutional compression.

But FSP’s compression target differs.

Simon compresses primarily for:

> **tractability and explanation.**

FSP compresses primarily for:

> **governability without semantic overreach.**

The overlap is substantial.

The purpose differs.

# 36. What FSP Does Not Claim Against Simon

The comparison must avoid caricature.

FSP does **not** claim:

- Simon ignored norms;
- Simon treated design as value-free;
- Simon believed all optimization was legitimate;
- Simon ignored organizations or authority;
- Simon denied uncertainty;
- Simon assumed exhaustive search;
- Simon treated hierarchy as metaphysical finality.

The narrower claim is:

> Simon’s central formal achievements concern bounded adaptation, procedural rationality, search, design, and complex organization. FSP’s constitutional focus is instead on the governance of crossings among partial representations, claims, scales, roles, authorities, and consequences.

This difference in center of gravity is enough.

> **Both Simon and FSP seek a level of description abstract enough to travel across domains but concrete enough to preserve the structure relevant to action.**

Simon finds this in:

- bounded procedure;
- search;
- hierarchy;
- interface;
- design.

FSP finds it in:

- relation;
- transition;
- burden;
- ceiling;
- remainder;
- Return.

# 39. Strongest Difference

The strongest difference is:

> **Simon’s portable architecture is primarily about tractable adaptation and design under bounded rationality; FSP’s portable architecture is primarily about governing what may be carried across partial maps into claims, authorization, action, consequence, and renewed inquiry.**

This is the comparator’s central result.

# 40. Near-Decomposability as a Foundation for Compression

Simon helps FSP answer a question the constitutional build left open:

> Why should compression work at all?

One answer is:

> Because many complex systems and inquiry structures are sufficiently near-decomposable that local relations can be modeled without representing every coupling at equal resolution.

But FSP adds:

> **Compression is justified only relative to the claim and transition being governed.**

Thus:

```text
near-decomposability
→ possibility of tractable compression

bridge governance
→ conditions for responsible recomposition
```

This is a major synthesis.

---

# 41. Boundary Condition: Strong Coupling

Simon’s near-decomposability also gives FSP a failure condition.

If a domain has:

- dense strong coupling;
- rapid cross-level feedback;
- unstable boundaries;
- pervasive path dependence;
- nonlocal consequence;

then compression may fail.

FSP should therefore ask:

> **Is the system decomposable enough for this map and this claim?**

This should become a future decompression trigger.

But it does not yet require a new constitutional object.

---

# 42. Boundary Condition: Changing Goals

Simon’s design framework often begins with goals or preference criteria.

FSP must additionally govern goal change.

Goals may be:

- inherited;
- institutionally imposed;
- contested;
- historically assembled;
- morally defective;
- mutually incompatible.

Therefore:

> **Given goal ≠ justified goal.**

This is another governance distinction that should remain explicit.

---

# 43. Boundary Condition: Multiple Stakeholders

A single-agent search model can become inadequate when:

- multiple actors have different goals;
- authority is distributed;
- standing differs from power;
- one actor’s solution is another’s enclosure;
- consequences are asymmetric.

FSP’s standing and authorization layers become essential here.

Thus:

> **Multi-agent design ≠ single-agent search with more variables.**

---

# 44. Boundary Condition: Ontology

Simon’s functional abstraction can often bracket ontology.

FSP agrees when the task permits.

But some claims explicitly concern:

- what exists;
- what kind of entity is present;
- what counts as a person;
- what constitutes consciousness;
- what level is causally relevant.

Then ontology becomes decision-relevant.

FSP’s rule is:

> **Use no more ontology than the task requires, but do not hide ontology when the claim depends on it.**

---

# 45. Boundary Condition: Phenomenology

Simon’s framework is not designed to solve the first-person/third-person problem.

FSP’s Objective Phenomenology work demonstrates why governance must sometimes preserve an irreducible or presently unresolved remainder.

Thus:

> **Functional adaptation ≠ phenomenal identity.**

This is one of the clearest cases where Simonian interface analysis is useful but insufficient for the target claim.

---

# 46. Boundary Condition: Moral Standing

Similarly:

> **Functional contribution ≠ moral standing.**

This matters for:

- animals;
- AI;
- impaired humans;
- synthetic agents;
- institutions.

FSP therefore separates agent role from normative profile.

# 48. Research Hypothesis

The comparator motivates a new but bounded research hypothesis:

This should remain a hypothesis.

# 49. Compression/Recomposition Failure Taxonomy

A future research program could distinguish:

## Type A — Bad decomposition

Relevant coupling omitted.

## Type B — Bad target binding

Subsystem or target incorrectly specified.

## Type C — Bad recomposition

Local result carried across boundary without license.

## Type D — Bad authorization

Correct result used by an actor without standing or authority.

## Type E — Bad Return

Consequences fail to update the next inquiry state.

This taxonomy is promising because it joins Simon’s complexity architecture to FSP governance without conflating them.

# 56. Comparator Firewalls

The following firewalls govern future use of Simon in FSP.

> **Structural affinity ≠ intellectual derivation.**

> **Satisficing ≠ claim ceiling.**

> **Near-decomposability ≠ safe independence.**

> **Functional equivalence ≠ ontological identity.**

> **Search-space reachability ≠ authorization.**

> **Preferred-state reachability ≠ legitimacy.**

> **Procedural rationality ≠ full governance adequacy.**

> **Artificial participation ≠ moral standing.**

> **Compression for tractability ≠ compression licensed for every claim.**

These should remain comparator firewalls unless later regression justifies promotion.

# 57. Strongest Supported Claim

> **Simon provides one of the strongest historical and conceptual comparators for FSP because his work shows how bounded agents, artificial systems, organizations, and complex hierarchies can be understood through selective representation, satisficing, search, interface structure, design, and near-decomposability. FSP occupies a different but adjacent problem space: it governs the transitions by which partial representations become claims, coordinated judgments, authorizations, interventions, consequences, and renewed inquiry. Near-decomposability helps explain why compression is possible; FSP’s bridge governance explains when recomposition is responsible.**

This build does not establish:

# 59. REOPEN Conditions

Reopen this comparator if:

1. closer Simon scholarship shows that the governance gap is substantially narrower than represented here;
2. FSP cases fail to distinguish satisficing from bounded judgment;
3. near-decomposability does not help explain successful constitutional compression;
4. strong-coupling cases require a different complexity comparator;
5. Simon’s organizational work supports a richer standing/authority comparison;
6. later research shows that problem-space construction already handles more of FSP’s map-governance burden than this build credits;
7. the comparator becomes genealogical rhetoric rather than a genuine structural test.

# 62. Final Statement

Simon gives us a powerful answer to one question:

> **How can finite systems cope with complexity?**

FSP asks a second:

> **Once complexity has been compressed enough to permit inquiry and action, how do we govern the crossings that compression makes possible?**

The synthesis is:

```text
NEAR-DECOMPOSABILITY
→ TRACTABLE PARTIAL MAPS
→ BOUNDED SEARCH / DESIGN
→ GOVERNED TRANSITIONS
→ CLAIM CEILINGS / REMAINDERS
→ AUTHORIZED ACTION
→ CONSEQUENCE
→ REOPEN
→ RETURN
```

The deepest shared insight is that intelligent inquiry does not require total representation.

The deepest FSP addition is:

> **Partial representation creates a responsibility to govern what crosses its boundaries.**
