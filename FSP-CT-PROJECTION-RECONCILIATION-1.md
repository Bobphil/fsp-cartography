# FSP-CT-PROJECTION-RECONCILIATION-1

## Side-by-Side Reconciliation of the History of Ethics and History of Inquiry Governed Critical Thinking Introductions

**Status:** PROJECTION RECONCILIATION COMPLETE  
**Inputs:** FSP-CT-ETHICS-1; FSP-CT-INQUIRY-1  
**Canonical source:** FSP-CT-CANONICAL-1.1 — FROZEN  
**Purpose:** Identify the shared minimum, preserve domain-native differences, reduce student-facing overload, and specify the actual Pressbook placement target for each projection.  
**Rule:** Compression may reduce exposition; it may not mutate canonical meaning.

---

# 1. Executive Decision

Both projections are conceptually sound.

Neither requires conceptual repair.

Both are too large for the role they are supposed to play in the Pressbooks.

The problem is not that either text contains the wrong ideas. The problem is **information timing**.

The introductions currently contain material that belongs in three different places:

1. **orientation material** that students need before the course begins;
2. **chapter-level material** that students should encounter when a philosopher or case makes the distinction meaningful;
3. **deep-reference material** that belongs in the FSP Encyclopedia.

The Pressbook introductions should contain only the first category plus a light preview of the second.

The canonical Encyclopedia remains the place where the full architecture lives.

The reconciliation therefore recommends:

> **Compress both introductions to a common six-part student-facing shell, then let the chapters progressively disclose the domain-specific Tool ecology.**

The shared shell should be:

```text
1. You are already an inquirer.
2. Your first map is partial.
3. Critical thinking tests reasons and evidence.
4. Governed inquiry also tests transitions around the argument.
5. Use a compact six-question routine.
6. Preserve a claim ceiling and REOPEN condition.
```

Everything else should be selectively routed into chapters, cases, sidebars, or the Encyclopedia.

---

# 2. What Must Remain Common

The two Pressbooks should visibly belong to the same FSP family.

They therefore need a common conceptual minimum.

## 2.1 Situated inquiry

Both introductions should begin from the same human starting point:

> Human beings are already situated, fallible inquirers before they encounter formal philosophy or formal science.

This is the deepest shared entry.

The Ethics version then turns toward moral situations.

The Inquiry version turns toward observation, measurement, explanation, and revision.

But the starting point should remain recognizably the same.

---

## 2.2 Partial maps

Both introductions should preserve:

> **Map ≠ World.**

The Ethics version should usually retain the fuller:

> **Map ≠ World ≠ Person.**

because representation of other persons is central to moral judgment.

The Inquiry version does not need to foreground Map ≠ Person in its opening, though the principle remains available in historical reconstruction and social inquiry.

The shared student habit is:

> **Before reasoning confidently, inspect what has entered the map and what has been left out.**

---

## 2.3 Traditional critical thinking is preserved

Both projections should explicitly say that FSP does not replace:

- argument analysis;
- deduction and induction;
- abduction;
- analogy;
- evidence evaluation;
- causal reasoning;
- source criticism;
- counterexamples;
- fallacy analysis;
- revision in light of reasons.

But this can be one compact paragraph rather than a long inventory.

The shared claim remains:

> **FSP extends rather than replaces traditional critical thinking.**

---

## 2.4 Tool ≠ Verdict

Both Pressbooks need this sentence.

Students must know that the recurring questions do not automatically determine the answer.

> **Tool ≠ Verdict.**

This is especially important because both courses deliberately pair historical material with modern cases.

---

## 2.5 Claim ceilings

Both introductions should teach one recurring habit:

> **What does not yet follow?**

This may be the most portable FSP habit across domains.

The examples should differ by domain, but the discipline should remain common.

---

## 2.6 REOPEN

Both courses should introduce REOPEN before the first major case.

The shared formulation can be short:

> **What evidence, consequence, failed expectation, changed condition, or better explanation would make us reconsider?**

Ethics will add affected persons, standing, legitimacy, and harm.

Inquiry will add anomalies, measurements, predictions, models, and source revisions.

---

## 2.7 Historical reconstruction firewall

Both Pressbooks must preserve:

> **Historical actor ≠ FSP reconstruction.**

For Ethics:

> philosopher / text ≠ Tool label.

For Inquiry:

> historical episode / investigator ≠ universal FSP method.

This is necessary because both books deliberately perform historical-to-present transfer.

---

## 2.8 Return to human authorship

Both courses should preserve a light version of Authorial Return.

Students do not need the entire constitutional treatment in the introduction.

They do need the practical rule:

> **The framework helps you inspect the inquiry; it does not make the final judgment for you.**

The phrase **Cognitive support ≠ outsourced authorship** can remain in a sidebar or end note rather than the central prose if desired.

---

# 3. What Should Remain Domain-Specific

Shared grammar should not create identical introductions.

## Ethics must foreground

The Ethics Pressbook should give priority to:

- persons and moral visibility;
- formation of character and judgment;
- responsibility under constraint;
- moral authority;
- legitimacy;
- respect and autonomy;
- consequences and distribution;
- power and background conditions;
- fairness;
- capability;
- judgment versus authorization;
- connected Doing through institutions.

Its distinctive governing question is:

> **What should we judge, what does that judgment authorize, and how should it affect the lives and capabilities of persons?**

---

## Inquiry must foreground

The History of Inquiry Pressbook should give priority to:

- observation;
- measurement;
- instrumentation;
- representation;
- hypothesis and model;
- inferential license;
- alternative explanations;
- auxiliary assumptions;
- model change;
- conceptual change;
- source ledgers;
- institutional validation;
- anomaly;
- revision.

Its distinctive governing question is:

> **What did investigators observe, how was it represented, what did the evidence license, and what forced inquiry to change?**

---

# 4. Side-by-Side Core Matrix

| Shared function | History of Ethics expression | History of Inquiry expression |
|---|---|---|
| Situated inquiry | We already navigate people, rules, consequences, loyalties, and uncertainty | We already notice patterns, compare, predict, use tools, and revise |
| Map audit | What situation and persons have entered the map? | What observation, measurement, or representation entered the inquiry? |
| Assembly history | Habits, values, institutions, power, character | Concepts, instruments, methods, institutions, accepted standards |
| Reasons | Why should I believe or judge this? | What licenses the inference from evidence to claim? |
| Alternatives | What other ethical interpretation or course of action remains? | What rival explanation, model, or auxiliary assumption remains? |
| Standing | Who may decide, constrain, recommend, or intervene? | Who may certify, interpret, publish, or act on this knowledge claim? |
| Institutional amplification | Rules, procedures, policies, professional practices | Labs, journals, observatories, collaborations, review, databases |
| Claim ceiling | What does this moral argument not yet justify? | What does this evidence/model not yet establish? |
| REOPEN | What new harm, voice, consequence, or challenge matters? | What anomaly, failed prediction, new measurement, or better model matters? |
| Return | Authored ethical judgment | Authored epistemic/historical judgment |

This is the common family resemblance that should remain visible after compression.

# 5. Ethics Draft — What to Keep

The following material should remain in the actual Pressbook introduction.

## Keep fully or nearly fully

### A. Opening human situation

The opening paragraphs beginning with the fact that ethics does not start with a philosophy book are strong and course-native.

Keep the idea that ordinary human life already contains inquiry.

### B. Partial maps

Keep:

> **Map ≠ World ≠ Person.**

Keep the short question:

> What happened? What am I adding? What might I be missing?

### C. Assembly history

Keep the concept, but compress it to two paragraphs.

Keep both:

> **Formation ≠ justification.**

and:

> **Origin ≠ validity.**

These distinctions are pedagogically important for Aristotle and for Marx/Nietzsche later.

### D. Governed ethical inquiry

Keep the compact recursive arc:

```text
situation
↔ map
↔ reasons
↔ judgment
↔ standing / authorization
↔ action
↔ consequences
↔ REOPEN
```

### E. Six-question compact routine

Keep essentially intact.

This should become the practical center of the introduction.

### F. Judgment / authorization distinction

Keep, but compress to one strong section.

This is a major distinctive contribution of the Ethics projection.

### G. Claim ceilings and REOPEN

Keep, but reduce the long list of examples.

### H. Final course-return paragraph

Keep the thesis that the history of ethics expands what ethical inquiry can notice.

This is the best bridge into the historical sequence.

# 6. Ethics Draft — What to Move Out of the Introduction

## Move the complete Twelve-Tool list

The full “Twelve Recurring Ethical Questions” section is useful, but it is too much before students have encountered the philosophers.

Recommended placement:

- short statement in the introduction: “Across the course you will acquire twelve recurring questions.”
- full Tool Rack in the Interlude or reference appendix;
- each Tool introduced in its chapter;
- Encyclopedia link for the full canonical version.

## Move the twelve-chapter chapter-by-chapter preview

The complete twelve-chapter ethical arc duplicates the actual book and creates premature cognitive load.

Replace it with one short paragraph naming three phases:

1. attention, reasons, formation, and agency;
2. authority, perspective, respect, and consequence;
3. power, fairness, capability, and durable cooperation.

The full chapter mapping belongs in faculty-facing architecture or course navigation, not the student introduction.

## Move the major-case catalog

Boisjoly, Hanna, Mandela, Gulf Pines, FlexWorks, Commonwealth Colleges, and Maathai should appear when their chapters arrive.

The introduction needs only one sentence:

> “Real cases will pressure-test these questions throughout the book.”

## Compress institutional amplification

Keep the idea that judgments become rules and practices.

Move the extended institutional diagnostic questions into the Power & Flourishing Lab or later institutional chapters.

## Compress Authorial Return terminology

Keep the practical idea.

The technical name “Authorial Return” can appear once, probably near the end, rather than functioning as a separate major section.

# 7. Ethics Over-Instrumentation Judgment

**Current state:** MODERATELY OVER-INSTRUMENTED.

Why?

The draft currently asks the introduction to do four jobs:

- orient the student;
- teach the full Tool Rack;
- preview all twelve chapters;
- preview all major cases.

Those last three jobs are already performed elsewhere in the course.

The introduction should instead create a durable **first mental model**.

Recommended target:

> **1,800–2,500 words**, approximately 4–6 Pressbook pages depending on formatting.

The current draft should be compressed by roughly **40–50%** for actual student placement.

No conceptual material needs to be deleted from the course ecosystem; it should be redistributed.

# 8. Inquiry Draft — What to Keep

## Keep fully or nearly fully

### A. Inquiry begins before science has a name

This is an excellent domain-native opening.

Keep the movement from ordinary human inquiry to formal scientific practices.

### B. Situated observation

Keep the idea that observation depends on instruments, capacities, learned distinctions, and procedures.

Keep:

> **Map ≠ World.**

### C. Instrument mediation

Keep a short version of:

> **Instrument reading ≠ uninterpreted fact.**

This is genuinely domain-specific and worth introducing early.

### D. Assembly history of inquiry

Keep the concept, but compress.

It is especially useful for explaining inherited concepts, instruments, practices, and standards.

Keep:

> **Origin ≠ validity.**

### E. Six-question compact inquiry routine

Keep essentially intact.

This should be the practical center of the Inquiry introduction.

### F. Model claim ceiling

Keep:

> **Model success ≠ ontology.**

This is one of the strongest inquiry-domain firewalls.

### G. Source-ledger distinction

Keep a compact version:

> **Historical source ≠ scholarly reconstruction ≠ contemporary scientific source ≠ FSP reconstruction.**

This is essential to the History of Inquiry project.

### H. REOPEN

Keep.

It is central to the course's diachronic structure.

### I. Course-return paragraph

Keep the idea that history of inquiry records changing ways human beings make the world answer back.

# 9. Inquiry Draft — What to Move Out of the Introduction

## Move Duhem–Quine detail

The introduction currently explains the auxiliary-hypothesis problem through Neptune/Vulcan.

That material is excellent, but it belongs in the Duhem–Quine chapter or case dossier.

The introduction only needs:

> Evidence can constrain inquiry without uniquely identifying which assumption must be revised.

## Move Kuhn and meaning-change detail

The current section on meaning change belongs in the Kuhn chapter.

The introduction can simply preview:

> Frameworks can affect what questions, concepts, and standards are available.

## Move thought-experiment theory

The controlled-revision-space account is important but should be taught when the thought-experiment chapter arrives.

The introduction only needs to signal that inquiry can test ideas through experiments, models, and controlled imagined cases.

## Move the complete core-case arc

Galileo, Foucault, Neptune/Vulcan, Newton→Einstein, Duhem–Quine, Kuhn, thought experiments, and DESI should not all be summarized up front.

Replace with one sentence:

> “The course will test this framework through historical episodes ranging from Galileo and Foucault to Neptune/Vulcan, relativity, Kuhn, thought experiments, and DESI.”

## Compress institutional science

Keep one paragraph explaining that knowledge becomes durable through communities and institutions.

Move detailed questions about journals, funding, collaborations, admissibility, and consensus into later chapters or Atlas dossiers.

## Compress epistemic authorization

Keep the distinction but do not create a full separate early theory of epistemic standing.

One paragraph is sufficient.

# 10. Inquiry Over-Instrumentation Judgment

**Current state:** CLEARLY OVER-INSTRUMENTED FOR AN INTRODUCTION, though not conceptually bloated.

That weakens the historical discovery structure of the book.

Recommended target:

Again, the removed material should remain in the course ecosystem.

# 11. Shared Pressbook Introduction Shell

The actual student-facing introductions should now use the same six-part architecture.

## Part 1 — You are already an inquirer

One short domain-native opening.

Ethics: navigating persons, rules, uncertainty, consequences.

Inquiry: noticing patterns, measuring, explaining, revising.

## Part 2 — Your first map is partial

Introduce the relevant map firewall.

Ethics:

> Map ≠ World ≠ Person.

Inquiry:

> Map ≠ World.

## Part 3 — Critical thinking tests reasons

One paragraph preserving traditional CT.

Then explain the FSP extension:

> good reasoning can still operate on an incomplete representation or support a conclusion whose broader standing remains unsettled.

## Part 4 — Governed inquiry asks what kind of work is needed

Give one short recursive arc.

No twelve-Tool inventory here.

## Part 5 — Six questions you can use now

This becomes the student's operational entry.

### Ethics six

1. What is my map?
2. What are the reasons?
3. What shaped the situation?
4. Who is affected and who has standing?
5. What is my claim ceiling?
6. What would REOPEN this?

### Inquiry six

1. What was observed or made measurable?
2. What inference was licensed?
3. What alternatives remained?
4. What shaped the inquiry?
5. What is the claim ceiling?
6. What would REOPEN this?

## Part 6 — What the history will add

One short closing paragraph.

Ethics:

> philosophers progressively enlarge what moral inquiry can notice.

Inquiry:

> historical episodes progressively enlarge what inquiry can observe, represent, test, explain, and revise.

This six-part shell should become the stable Pressbook projection format.

# 12. Progressive Disclosure Architecture

The reconciliation recommends the following distribution.

## Layer 1 — Pressbook Introduction

Student needs:

- situated inquiry;
- map firewall;
- traditional CT retained;
- governed-inquiry idea;
- six-question routine;
- claim ceiling;
- REOPEN;
- one sentence on historical transfer.

## Layer 2 — Individual Chapters

Student encounters:

- chapter-specific Tool;
- philosopher or historical episode;
- case;
- distinctive failure mode;
- chapter-specific claim ceiling;
- transfer task.

## Layer 3 — Interlude / Reference Rack

Student can inspect:

- all twelve Tools;
- Failure Atlas;
- cross-chapter comparison;
- Tool sequencing;
- cumulative Tool Rack.

## Layer 4 — FSP Encyclopedia

Reader can inspect:

- canonical definitions;
- constitutional firewalls;
- comparator notes;
- assembly-history foundations;
- full failure routing;
- institutional amplification;
- source ledgers;
- formal architecture;
- cross-domain projections.

This is the correct implementation of:

> **Progressive disclosure ≠ semantic mutation.**

# 15. Pressbook Placement Recommendation

## History of Ethics

Place the compressed governed-critical-thinking introduction:

> **after the general course/book orientation and before Chapter 1 Attention.**

It should prepare the reader to understand why Chapter 1 begins with attention rather than with a moral theory.

A short “You will build the full Tool Rack as the course proceeds” note should point forward.

# 16. Reconciliation Decision

Both projections **PASS conceptually**.

Both require **pedagogical compression before Pressbook insertion**.

The target is not to make the two introductions identical.

The target is:

> **same governed-inquiry skeleton, different domain-native pressure points.**

The reconciled distinction is:

```text
COMMON CORE
situated inquiry
→ partial map
→ reasons / evidence
→ bounded judgment
→ claim ceiling
→ REOPEN

ETHICS SPECIALIZATION
persons

→ standing / legitimacy
→ consequences / distribution

INQUIRY SPECIALIZATION
observation

```

## Final claim ceiling

This reconciliation establishes a pedagogical division of labor.

It does not show that every student needs the same amount of FSP language, that every course should expose the same Tool vocabulary, or that domain-specific chapters must follow one fixed order.

Its bounded conclusion is:

> **The frozen FSP critical-thinking architecture can support both Pressbooks with a common student-facing minimum, but the current drafts expose too much of the deeper machinery too early. The correct repair is progressive disclosure: keep the shared governed-inquiry skeleton in the introduction, teach domain-specific Tools through the chapters and cases, and leave the full architecture to the Encyclopedia.**
