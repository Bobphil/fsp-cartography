# FSP-PRESSBOOK-RECONCILIATION-1-AUTH-PROGRESSION

## Later-Ethics Authorization Progression — Chapters 5, 6, 8, and 9

**Status:** IMPLEMENTATION PACKAGE  
**Source contract:** FSP-PRESSBOOK-RECONCILIATION-1  
**Pilot precedent:** FSP-PRESSBOOK-RECONCILIATION-1-PILOTS-A  
**Targets:** Ethics Chapters 5, 6, 8, 9  
**Primary purpose:** Introduce authorization progressively without displacing each chapter's historical/philosophical center.  
**Workload rule:** No ordinary workbook should exceed the existing 60-minute target.  
**Projection rule:** Authorization is a later-course governance distinction, not a new master topic.

---

# 1. Progression hypothesis

The Ethics course should not introduce the full action-state grammar at once.

Instead, authorization develops across four chapters:

```text
Ch5
GOOD PURPOSE ≠ SUFFICIENT AUTHORIZATION

↓ broaden

Ch6
POWER / LEGITIMACY ≠ AUTHORIZATION OF ANY RESPONSE

↓ deepen

Ch8
HELP / MANAGEMENT ≠ AUTHORIZED AGENCY BYPASS

↓ integrate

Ch9
EXPECTED BENEFIT ≠ UNRESTRICTED AUTHORIZATION
```

The chapters therefore teach four different authorization pressures:

- **Chapter 5:** why a good end does not by itself license a specific means;
- **Chapter 6:** why authority, legitimacy, and resistance must remain distinct;
- **Chapter 8:** why intervention can be well-intentioned yet still violate agency;
- **Chapter 9:** why aggregate welfare does not by itself settle permission, burden, or liberty.

The progression culminates later in the full action-state grammar:

> **GENERATED → POSSIBLE → RECOMMENDED → AUTHORIZED → IMPLEMENTED → REVIEWED**

That full grammar belongs in the Power & Flourishing Lab, not in Chapter 5.

---

# 2. Shared authorization distinctions

Across Chapters 5, 6, 8, and 9, preserve:

> **Supported judgment ≠ unrestricted authorization.**

> **Generate ≠ Recommend ≠ Authorize.**

> **Power ≠ legitimacy.**

> **Benefit ≠ authorization.**

> **Legal permission ≠ complete moral justification.**

Do not require students to memorize these as a list.

Each chapter should foreground only the distinction needed for its own case.

# 3. Chapter 5 — Aquinas / Augustine

## Historical / philosophical center

Moral authority, law, common good, legitimate purpose, and the relation between rule and human good.

## Authorization pressure

A rule or intervention may pursue a genuine good without being uniquely or sufficiently justified.

Primary firewall:

> **Good purpose ≠ sufficient authorization.**

Secondary:

> **Common-good language ≠ automatic permission for every means used in its name.**

## Student-facing chapter addition

Add one short paragraph near the transition from historical material to case/application:

> A rule can aim at a real good and still require further justification. Once a reason has identified a valuable end, another question begins: **who may impose this particular rule, on whom, through which means, and under what limits?** Ethical reasoning therefore does not end when we identify a good purpose.

Do not introduce the full action-state grammar here.

## Workbook patch

Add one bounded move before the Output Card:

### From good end to permitted means

1. What good is the rule or action trying to secure?
2. Why does that good matter?
3. What specific means are being proposed?
4. What additional reason is needed to show that this means is permitted?

Then add:

> **A good end can support a reason for action without authorizing every available means.**

## Output Card terminal patch

### Bounded judgment

What good is genuinely at stake, and what rule or response is currently best supported?

### Claim ceiling

Complete:

> Because the proposed rule aims at a genuine good, it does **not** yet follow that ______________________________.

### Authorization boundary

Who has standing to impose, apply, or enforce this rule?

What limit should constrain that authority?

### REOPEN

What evidence of burden, misuse, changed circumstances, or failed common-good justification would require review?

### Return

> The next responsible move is to ______________________________ while preserving ______________________________.

## Time ceiling

6–8 minutes.

## Chapter 5 acceptance test

PASS only if:
- students still understand Aquinas/common-good reasoning;
- authorization is introduced as a transition problem, not as the chapter's primary doctrine;
- no student is required to learn legal institutional theory here.

# 4. Chapter 6 — Social Contract / Legitimacy

## Historical / philosophical center

Authority, political obligation, legitimacy, consent, standing, resistance.

## Authorization pressure

This is the first chapter where authorization should become explicit.

Primary firewalls:

> **Power ≠ legitimacy.**

> **Illegitimacy ≠ automatic authorization of any resistance.**

The chapter must distinguish:
- whether an institution may rule;
- whether a rule is legitimate;
- whether a person may resist;
- which forms of resistance are justified.

## Student-facing chapter addition

Add near the chapter's contemporary transfer:

> Showing that an institution lacks legitimacy does not finish the ethical inquiry. A second question follows: **what responses are now justified, by whom, and under what constraints?** The failure of one authority does not create unlimited authority in everyone else.

## Workbook patch

Add a **Legitimacy → Response Test**:

### Step A — Authority

What power does the institution actually possess?

### Step B — Legitimacy

What gives that power moral standing, if anything?

### Step C — Failure

What specific legitimacy condition is absent or violated?

### Step D — Response

Which responses become:
- possible;
- reasonable to recommend;
- morally authorized?

Do not assume these categories collapse.

## Mandela transfer

Mandela should become the major bridge case for:

- internal vs external strategy;
- legitimacy;
- resistance;
- refusal;
- cooperation without convergence.

Add:

> **Cooperation ≠ obligation to cooperate.**

and:

> **Joint task ≠ legitimate task.**

These should appear in instructor-facing notes and, if student-facing, only where the case makes them concrete.

## Output Card terminal patch

### Bounded judgment

Is the authority or rule legitimate under the reasons examined?

### Claim ceiling

Complete:

> Even if the authority is illegitimate, it does **not** automatically follow that ______________________________.

### Authorization boundary

Name one response that is:
- justified to consider;
- justified to recommend;
- justified to authorize.

Explain why those may differ.

### Remaining remainder

Whose standing or burden could still change the legitimacy analysis?

### REOPEN

What institutional reform, changed consent condition, new burden, or altered power relation would reopen your judgment?

### Return

> What should the affected person or group responsibly do next, given both the legitimacy judgment and the limits on response?

## Time ceiling

7–9 minutes.

## Chapter 6 acceptance test

PASS only if:
- legitimacy remains the chapter's center;
- resistance is not treated as automatically justified;
- students can distinguish "authority fails" from "anything goes."

# 5. Chapter 8 — Kant

## Historical / philosophical center

Respect, duty, autonomy, persons as ends, agency.

## Authorization pressure

Intervention may be intended to help yet still bypass or replace another person's agency.

Primary firewall:

> **Help / management ≠ authorized agency bypass.**

Secondary:

> **Respect for autonomy ≠ automatic deference to every present preference.**

This chapter should clarify that authorization is not merely institutional permission. It can also concern whether one person is morally entitled to act over or around another person's agency.

## Student-facing chapter addition

Add near the Leila / adviser or analogous case transfer:

> A person may have strong reasons to think another person would benefit from a particular choice. But a benefit judgment does not settle whether one may decide for them. Kant sharpens the difference between **helping a person act** and **replacing the person as an agent**.

## Workbook patch

Add an **Agency-Boundary Test**:

1. What is the proposed intervention trying to achieve?
2. What role remains for the person's own judgment?
3. Is information being supplied, pressure being applied, choice being constrained, or choice being replaced?
4. What justification would be required for crossing that boundary?

Use plain language first.

Do not require legal consent doctrine unless already part of the case.

## Output Card terminal patch

### Bounded judgment

What does respect for the person require in this case?

### Claim ceiling

Complete:

> Because I judge that this action would help the person, it does **not** yet follow that ______________________________.

### Authorization boundary

Which of the following is currently justified?

- inform;
- advise;
- persuade;
- constrain;
- decide for;
- none of the above.

Explain the boundary.

### Remaining remainder

What aspect of the person's agency, capacity, dependency, or relationship is least well represented in the current map?

### REOPEN

What change in capacity, risk, consent, coercion, or information would make you revise the intervention boundary?

### Return

> What action best preserves the person as an agent while responding to the actual ethical problem?

## Time ceiling

6–8 minutes.

## Chapter 8 acceptance test

PASS only if:
- Kant remains about respect/duty/autonomy;
- students do not equate autonomy with simple preference satisfaction;
- authorization deepens agency analysis rather than replacing it.

# 6. Chapter 9 — Bentham / Mill

## Historical / philosophical center

Consequences, welfare, harm, liberty, distribution, uncertainty.

## Authorization pressure

This is the integration chapter where authorization becomes visibly downstream of consequence analysis.

Primary firewall:

> **Aggregate benefit ≠ unrestricted authorization.**

Secondary:

> **Expected welfare gain ≠ permission to ignore distribution, liberty, or uncertainty.**

## Student-facing chapter addition

Add near the chapter's major case:

> Consequence analysis can show that one option is expected to produce more benefit or less harm. But that is not yet the same as showing that any actor may impose it in any form. Once consequences are estimated, ethical inquiry still has to ask who bears the burden, whose liberty is constrained, how uncertain the prediction is, and who has standing to authorize the intervention.

## Workbook patch

Use the Pilot-A Ch9 Output Card as the canonical terminal structure.

Before the Output Card add one short **Benefit-to-Authorization Bridge**:

### Benefit-to-Authorization Bridge

1. Which option has the strongest expected welfare case?
2. Who bears the largest burden?
3. Whose liberty is constrained?
4. How certain is the predicted benefit?
5. Who may authorize implementation?
6. What review condition should accompany the decision?

Do not turn this into a second full case analysis.

## Output Card

Use:

- bounded judgment;
- claim ceiling;
- authorization boundary;
- remaining remainder;
- REOPEN;
- Return.

Canonical claim ceiling:

> **Even if this option produces the largest expected benefit, it does not yet follow that every burden, restriction, or means of implementation is justified.**

## Time ceiling

7–9 minutes total for bridge + Output Card.

## Chapter 9 acceptance test

PASS only if:
- students perform consequence analysis before authorization analysis;
- authorization does not become a substitute for welfare reasoning;
- burden distribution and liberty remain visible.

|---|---|---|---|
| Ch5 Aquinas | What good should law/rule serve? | Does this good authorize this means? | Good purpose ≠ sufficient authorization |

# 8. What students should know by the end of Chapter 9

Students should be able to distinguish:

```text
a reason for believing
≠
a reason for recommending
≠
a reason for authorizing
≠
evidence of successful implementation
```

They do not yet need the formal labels:

```text
GENERATED → POSSIBLE → RECOMMENDED → AUTHORIZED → IMPLEMENTED → REVIEWED
```

The full grammar is revealed later in the Power & Flourishing Lab.

Pedagogical rule:

> **Acquire the distinctions before naming the architecture.**

Each chapter should use one short authorization turn, not a new lecture block.

## Ch5

After historical/common-good work:
- 8–10 minutes: "Does a good end authorize this means?"

## Ch6

After legitimacy analysis:
- 10–12 minutes: "What follows for resistance?"

## Ch8

After Kantian respect/autonomy:
- 8–10 minutes: "Help, persuasion, or agency bypass?"

- 10–12 minutes: "Benefit-to-authorization bridge."

Keep the rest of each class domain-native.

# 11. Quiz guidance

Do not quiz students on the word "authorization" as vocabulary alone.

Use discrimination questions.

## Ch5 sample pattern

A policy promotes a genuine public good. Which conclusion follows?

Correct structure:
- the good supplies a reason in favor;
- further justification is required for the means.

## Ch6 sample pattern

Evidence shows an institution lacks legitimacy. Which conclusion is best supported?

Correct structure:
- resistance may become justified;
- the form and scope still require reasons.

## Ch8 sample pattern

An adviser predicts a better outcome if a person follows the adviser's choice. What remains unresolved?

Correct structure:
- whether the adviser is justified in overriding the person's agency.

## Ch9 sample pattern

A policy has the highest expected aggregate benefit. Which question remains ethically necessary?

Correct structure:
- distribution, liberty, uncertainty, and authorization.

No Tool codes required.

# 13. Personal Ethics Journal firewall

No authorization table is added to the Journal.

Students may reflect on:

- a time they had power without legitimacy;
- a time help felt like control;
- whether good intentions can justify intrusion;
- what burdens are hidden by "best overall outcome";
- how their judgment changed.

Do not require:
- action-state labels;
- standing matrices;
- authorization categories.

Firewall:

> **Ethical reflection ≠ institutional decision form.**

# 16. Regression tests

The progression fails if any of the following occurs:

## Historical flattening

FAIL if:
- Aquinas becomes merely "authorization";
- Social Contract becomes merely "resistance";
- Kant becomes merely "consent";
- Mill becomes merely "policy permission."

## Workload inflation

FAIL if:
- the chapter workbook exceeds the 60-minute target;
- authorization introduces a second full case.

## Semantic collapse

FAIL if students treat:
- good purpose = permission;
- illegitimacy = unlimited resistance;
- benefit = authorization;
- autonomy = preference satisfaction.

## Journal contamination

FAIL if authorization matrices enter the Personal Ethics Journal.

## Premature architecture

FAIL if students are required to memorize the full action-state grammar before PFLAB.

## Domain fidelity

PASS only if each chapter can still be identified primarily by its historical/philosophical problem without FSP terminology.

# 18. Final implementation verdict

The authorization progression should be implemented as four different chapter-native pressures rather than one repeated framework lesson.

The cumulative result is:

```text
Ch5
good purpose → permitted means

Ch6
legitimacy → bounded response

Ch8
beneficence / respect → agency boundary

Ch9
welfare judgment → authorization / burden / review
```

By the end of Chapter 9, students should be able to recognize:

> **A supported ethical judgment still leaves a further question: what, if anything, does this judgment authorize someone to do?**

That prepares Chapters 10–12 and the Power & Flourishing Lab without displacing the historical ethics course.
