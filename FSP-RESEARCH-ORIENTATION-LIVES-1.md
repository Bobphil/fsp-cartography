# Lives in Transformation

## Biography, Situated Agency, Historical Change, and the Question of What May Travel to Us

**Status:** FORMALIZED RESEARCH ORIENTATION — SOURCE MODEL READY / CASE DOSSIERS NOT YET PROMOTED  
**Research region:** Situated Agency and Transformation.

A biography is already a map.

It is assembled from surviving texts, testimony, actions, institutions, artifacts, later memories, and interpretation. A famous life can therefore become deceptively smooth: childhood, formation, crisis, achievement, legacy. Once the later significance of the person is known, earlier episodes are easily rewritten as steps toward an outcome that the earlier agent could not yet see.

FSP's Lives in Transformation architecture begins by resisting that hindsight compression.

The central object is not a heroic life story but a bounded diachronic agent map: a sequence of situated states connected by transformations. At each state the inquiry asks what capabilities, constraints, relationships, institutions, resources, commitments, and live possibilities were present. At each transition it distinguishes what the person wanted, expected, attempted, and actually produced.

This matters because:

**later significance ≠ earlier foresight**  
**failed plan ≠ failed transformation**  
**outcome success ≠ total success**

A plan may fail while producing new skills, relationships, standing, or options. A celebrated success may carry costs invisible in a conventional biography. Capability can increase in one dimension while decreasing in another.

The source problem is equally important. A statement in a letter, a public action, a contemporary report, an institutional record, a later memoir, and a biographer's interpretation do not provide the same kind of access. The public dossier must preserve those distinctions.

Therefore:

**biographical dossier ≠ person**  
**stated reason ≠ complete cause**  
**inferred cause ≠ demonstrated motive**

The architecture then asks a second question: what, if anything, may travel from another life to ours?

Historical education often moves too quickly from admiration to imitation. FSP instead treats exemplar portability as a governed transition. A learner should ask which structural relation appears relevant, how the contexts differ, what part of the analogy is warranted, what must not travel, and what reversible test could be used in the learner's own situation.

Hence:

> **Exemplar as mirror ≠ exemplar as template.**

This is why Lives in Transformation can connect historical research to Life Navigation without collapsing them. The historical dossier owns the reconstruction. The learner owns the personal judgment. Life Navigation is optional.

The public route is:

**PERSON → BIOGRAPHY MAP → TRANSFORMATION TIMELINE → CRITICAL TRANSITION → SOURCE LEDGER → INTENDED VS ACTUAL → CAPABILITY / CONSTRAINT DELTA → OPENED / CLOSED FUTURES → HISTORICAL RETURN → WHAT MIGHT TRAVEL? → WHAT MUST NOT TRAVEL? → SITUATED COMPARISON → OPTIONAL LIFE NAVIGATION**

### Claim ceiling

This orientation establishes an architecture for source-governed biographical transformation. It does not establish substantive claims about Aristotle, Mandela, Maathai, or any other pilot life until a sourced dossier is built.

### Research frontier

The next research task is empirical and historical: test whether the architecture preserves distinct life topologies, whether hindsight control improves historical judgment, whether capability trajectories add explanatory value, and whether exemplar portability reduces uncritical imitation.

### Source model

A future sourced dossier must distinguish:

**PRIMARY HISTORICAL EVIDENCE** — letters, speeches, diaries, official records, contemporary artifacts.  
**CONTEMPORARY TESTIMONY** — accounts by people present at the time.  
**LATER MEMORY / AUTOBIOGRAPHY** — retrospective self-account.  
**SCHOLARLY BIOGRAPHY / HISTORIOGRAPHY** — later reconstruction and interpretation.  
**FSP INFERENCE** — structural analysis added by the dossier.

Phase 3 will apply this model first to Maathai, then Aristotle and Mandela.
