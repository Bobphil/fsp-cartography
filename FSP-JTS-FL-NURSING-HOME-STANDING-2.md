# 1. Why “stakeholder” is too weak

The word **stakeholder** hides distinctions that FSP needs to preserve.

A person can be:

- deeply affected but have little institutional power;
- highly expert but lack authority to decide;
- legally authorized but poorly informed;
- operationally responsible but unable to change the governing rule;
- able to recommend but not authorize;
- able to authorize but not implement;
- able to implement but only within a bounded role.

Therefore:

> **Stake ≠ expertise ≠ standing ≠ authority ≠ practical power ≠ authorization.**

Layer 2 replaces the generic stakeholder model with a typed governance ledger.

---

# 2. Six distinct governance relations

## A. Evidential authority

What kind of claim is this participant especially qualified to support?

Examples:
- infection-control evidence;
- resident lived experience;
- staffing feasibility;
- regulatory interpretation;
- longitudinal family knowledge.

Firewall:

> **Expertise in one claim domain ≠ total evidential authority.**

## B. Moral standing

Whose interests, burdens, agency, dignity, or capabilities must count directly in the judgment?

Residents are not merely data sources.

Workers are not merely implementation resources.

Families are not merely visitors.

Moral standing can exist without formal decision authority.

## C. Legal / institutional authority

Who is empowered by law, regulation, policy, role, or delegated office to make a class of decisions?

This is role-bounded.

Firewall:

> **Institutional authority ≠ moral legitimacy in every use.**

## D. Recommendation standing

Who is entitled, by role or expertise, to recommend a course of action?

Recommendation standing can be strong even when authorization is absent.

Firewall:

> **Recommend ≠ authorize.**

## E. Authorization standing

Who may legitimately convert a recommendation into a binding action?

Authorization may be:
- resident-specific;
- clinical;
- administrative;
- regulatory;
- emergency-bounded;
- time-limited;
- conditional.

## F. Practical implementation power

Who can actually make the action happen?

A decision may be authorized yet impossible to implement safely.

Firewall:

> **Authorization ≠ effective capability.**

# 3. Canonical action-state grammar

For consequential actions, Gulf Pines uses:

```text
GENERATED
→ POSSIBLE
→ RECOMMENDED
→ AUTHORIZED
→ IMPLEMENTED
→ REVIEWED
```

Each transition requires a visible license.

A proposal may stop at any state.

Examples:

- "Allow essential caregiver visits" may be GENERATED by a family member.
- It becomes POSSIBLE after clinical and operational review.
- It becomes RECOMMENDED by the care team.
- It becomes AUTHORIZED by the appropriate facility decision-maker within resident-rights constraints.
- It becomes IMPLEMENTED by staff.
- It becomes REVIEWED after outcomes and burdens are observed.

Firewall:

> **Generated ≠ Possible ≠ Recommended ≠ Authorized ≠ Implemented ≠ Reviewed.**

# 4. Participant governance profiles

## Residents

### Evidential authority
Strongest regarding:
- pain;
- fear;
- loneliness;
- preferences;
- perceived burden;
- tolerance for isolation;
- meaning of home and relationship.

### Moral standing
Primary.

### Legal / institutional authority
Variable by decisional capacity, rights, consent status, and existing legal arrangements.

### Recommendation standing
Strong regarding personal care and burden.

### Authorization standing
Strongest over resident-specific choices where capacity and law permit.

### Practical power
Often limited by illness, dependence, cognition, mobility, communication, and institutional structure.

Key firewall:

> **Low practical power ≠ low moral standing.**

---

## Families / trusted supporters

### Evidential authority
Potentially strong regarding:
- resident baseline behavior;
- communication patterns;
- history;
- preferences;
- changes from normal functioning.

### Moral standing
Relational and significant, but distinct from the resident's own standing.

### Legal / institutional authority
Only when a valid proxy, guardian, surrogate, or other role applies.

### Recommendation standing
Strong as advocates and longitudinal informants.

### Authorization standing
Role-dependent.

### Practical power
Can provide communication, advocacy, informal care, and pressure, but often depends on facility access.

Key firewall:

> **Longitudinal knowledge ≠ automatic proxy authority.**

---

## Frontline staff

### Evidential authority
Strong regarding:
- shift-level reality;
- workflow;
- resident changes;
- feasibility;
- workload;
- informal adaptations;
- near misses.

### Moral standing
Directly affected workers with safety and professional-integrity interests.

### Legal / institutional authority
Role-bounded.

### Recommendation standing
Strong on feasibility and operational safety.

### Authorization standing
Usually limited except within delegated clinical/operational scope.

### Practical power
High for actual implementation, often low for policy redesign.

Key firewall:

> **Implementation responsibility ≠ policy authority.**

---

## Clinical leadership / infection prevention

### Evidential authority
Strong regarding:
- clinical deterioration;
- transmission;
- testing;
- infection-control practice;
- clinical risk.

### Moral standing
Professional obligations toward resident and worker health.

### Legal / institutional authority
Substantial in clinical domains, but not total over all facility governance.

### Recommendation standing
Strong for clinical/infection-control measures.

### Authorization standing
Varies by action and facility policy.

### Practical power
Depends on staffing and operational support.

Key firewall:

> **Clinical expertise ≠ total authorization.**

---

## Administration

### Evidential authority
Strong regarding:
- staffing allocation;
- contracting;
- logistics;
- organizational constraints;
- reporting workflows.

### Moral standing
Institutional responsibilities to residents, workers, families, and public obligations.

### Legal / institutional authority
High for facility operations within law and regulation.

### Recommendation standing
High for organizational policy.

### Authorization standing
High for many facility-wide actions, but bounded by rights, regulation, consent, professional duties, and evidence.

### Practical power
High but not unlimited.

Key firewall:

> **Operational authority ≠ moral completeness.**

---

## Regulators / public-health authorities

### Evidential authority
Strong regarding:
- population surveillance;
- public-health guidance;
- regulatory interpretation;
- compliance history.

### Moral standing
Public-interest responsibilities.

### Legal / institutional authority
High within jurisdiction.

### Recommendation standing
Strong in regulated/public-health domains.

### Authorization standing
Can mandate or prohibit specified actions within lawful scope.

### Practical power
Indirect; implementation remains local.

Key firewall:

> **Regulatory authority ≠ complete local knowledge.**

---

## Empirical analysts / data systems

### Evidential authority
Strong only within the quality and scope of the data and methods.

### Moral standing
None merely by virtue of computation.

### Legal / institutional authority
None by default.

### Recommendation standing
At most analytic/advisory.

### Authorization standing
None by default.

### Practical power
May be high if embedded into institutional systems; that increases governance risk.

Key firewall:

> **Predictive or analytic output ≠ decision authority.**

# 5. State-by-state standing map

## S0 — Early Signal

Primary governance question:

> Who may assess and initiate precaution before the cause is known?

Clinical/frontline evidence rises in importance.

Administration may activate bounded contingency procedures.

Resident standing remains fully present even though the immediate evidence question is clinical.

No participant has warrant to treat the entire facility as infected.

### S0 partition

- Clinical team: RECOMMENDATION standing rises.
- Administration: limited emergency AUTHORIZATION.
- Frontline staff: high feasibility and observation standing.
- Residents: primary moral standing; resident-specific consent where applicable.
- Families: informational standing.
- Regulators: background authority, not yet necessarily active.

Claim ceiling:

> **Precautionary authority ≠ unrestricted emergency power.**

---

## S1 — Widening Map

Primary governance question:

> Whose burden and feasibility constraints must now enter the decision?

Resident and family evidence gains practical significance.

Frontline feasibility becomes essential.

Clinical recommendation remains strong but no longer exhausts the problem.

### S1 partition

- Clinical leadership: strong recommendation.
- Residents: strong burden and preference standing.
- Families: stronger evidential input for resident-specific capability.
- Frontline staff: strong feasibility standing.
- Administration: coordination authority.
- Regulators: compliance boundary.

Claim ceiling:

> **Confirmed transmission ≠ authorization for one undifferentiated policy.**

---

## S2 — Intervention Pressure

Primary governance question:

> Who may impose consequential restrictions, and under what limits?

This is the strongest standing/authorization stress point.

### S2 partition

- Clinical leadership may recommend strong restrictions.
- Administration may authorize facility policy within law and rights constraints.
- Residents retain direct standing over imposed burdens.
- Proxies act only within valid legal roles.
- Families remain important but do not automatically authorize.
- Regulators may constrain available policy space.
- Frontline staff can reveal when an authorized policy is not safely implementable.

Crucial firewall:

> **Supported recommendation ≠ legitimate authorization.**

Second firewall:

> **Authorized policy ≠ ethically adequate implementation.**

---

## S3 — Revised Bounded Judgment

Primary governance question:

> Does the original authorization still hold after conditions change?

Emergency authority must not persist automatically.

### S3 partition

- Clinical leadership reassesses infection risk.
- Administration must reconsider policy scope.
- Resident preferences and capability become more action-guiding as emergency pressure declines.
- Frontline staff assess feasibility of differentiated reopening.
- Families regain greater relational role.
- Regulators remain a compliance boundary.

Crucial firewall:

> **Past authorization ≠ present authorization.**

And:

> **Emergency justification decays when its supporting conditions decay.**

# 6. Standing conflicts

Joint Task Spaces need not eliminate conflict.

They must make it inspectable.

## Conflict A — Clinical recommendation vs resident burden

Clinical leadership recommends restriction.

Resident experiences serious cognitive and relational harm.

FSP response:
- preserve both claims;
- identify distinct standing;
- seek alternative implementation;
- narrow claim ceiling;
- schedule review.

## Conflict B — Family advocacy vs resident preference

Family requests stricter protection.

Resident prefers contact and accepts some risk.

FSP response:
- distinguish family concern from resident authority;
- inspect capacity and legal role;
- preserve resident authorship where possible.

## Conflict C — Administration vs frontline feasibility

Administration authorizes a policy.

Frontline staff report it cannot be implemented safely.

FSP response:
- authorization remains visible;
- feasibility becomes a new constraint;
- policy returns to review.

## Conflict D — regulator requirement vs local optimization

Regulation mandates a minimum standard.

Local staff believe another configuration would be better.

FSP response:
- lawful floor remains binding;
- local design continues above/within that floor;
- local disagreement does not erase regulator standing.

# 7. Authorization audit

Before any consequential Gulf Pines action moves to AUTHORIZED, the Joint Task Space asks:

1. **What exactly is being authorized?**
2. **Who is affected?**
3. **Who recommended it?**
4. **What evidence supports the recommendation?**
5. **Who has authority to authorize it?**
6. **Is that authority role-bounded, emergency-bounded, resident-specific, or facility-wide?**
7. **What rights or standing constrain it?**
8. **Can it actually be implemented?**
9. **What burden does implementation create?**
10. **What is the claim ceiling?**
11. **When does authorization expire or require review?**
12. **What would REOPEN it?**

---

# 8. Authorization expiry

A key Layer-2 addition is **authorization expiry**.

Consequential authorization should carry a review condition.

Possible expiry modes:

- TIME_BOUND;
- EVIDENCE_BOUND;
- THRESHOLD_BOUND;
- CONDITION_BOUND;
- EVENT_BOUND;
- REVIEW_REQUIRED.

Example:

A visitation restriction may be:

```text
AUTHORIZED
scope: facility-wide
basis: active multi-unit transmission + staffing instability
review: 72 hours
REOPEN: case decline, staffing recovery, resident-harm threshold, new guidance
```

This prevents temporary emergency reasoning from sedimenting into default policy.

Firewall:

> **Authorization without review ≠ governed authorization.**

---

# 9. Standing provenance

Every standing claim should preserve its source.

Examples:

```text
standing_source:
- resident right
- professional role
- delegated authority
- statute / regulation
- institutional policy
- legal proxy status
- direct affected-party standing
- evidential expertise
```

Standing must not be inferred merely because someone is influential.

Firewall:

> **Practical power ≠ legitimate standing.**

# 10. Failure modes Layer 2 exposes

## Standing collapse
All participants treated as generic stakeholders.

## Expertise-authority collapse
The most expert person is assumed to be the authorized decision-maker.

## Power-legitimacy collapse
The actor who can impose a rule is treated as morally entitled to do so.

## Recommendation-authorization collapse
A recommendation becomes policy without an explicit authorization transition.

## Implementation erasure
A formally authorized rule ignores frontline feasibility.

## Emergency sedimentation
A temporary authorization persists after its justificatory conditions change.

## Proxy overreach
Family or representative standing exceeds its legal or moral scope.

## Resident erasure
Resident moral standing is reduced because practical power is low.

# 11. Layer-2 acceptance test

Layer 2 passes if the reader can distinguish:

1. who knows;
2. who is affected;
3. who may recommend;
4. who may authorize;
5. who can implement;
6. who must be consulted;
7. how these relations change across S0–S3;
8. when authorization expires;
9. how disagreement can persist without governance collapse;
10. why “stakeholder” is insufficient.

The core result is:

> **A Joint Task Space does not merely collect voices. It governs the relations among evidence, standing, recommendation, authorization, implementation, and review.**
