# 2. PUBLIC / QUICK — “Why FSP?”

## Purpose

Answer, in roughly one screen:

- What problem is FSP solving?
- What does FSP add to ordinary critical thinking?
- Why can it help people and domains work together?
- What is the key limit on its ambition?

## Public draft

### Why FSP?

Human inquiry does not happen only inside arguments.

We notice selectively, build partial maps, inherit categories, use specialized tools, reason from evidence, make judgments, act through institutions, and discover consequences. Problems often arise when the transitions between those activities become invisible.

Full-Stack Philosophy makes those transitions easier to inspect.

It keeps familiar critical-thinking practices—logic, evidence, inference, alternatives, source evaluation—and extends them with questions about representation, provenance, standing, authorization, consequences, repair, and reopening.

A compact way to remember the grammar is:

```text
Notice → Inspect → Govern → Bound → REOPEN → Return
```

- **Notice:** What entered the map, and what may be missing?
- **Inspect:** What reasons, evidence, alternatives, and Tools matter?
- **Govern:** Which transitions need provenance, standing, or justification?
- **Bound:** What does not yet follow?
- **REOPEN:** What would make us reconsider?
- **Return:** How does the inquiry return to responsible lived action?

FSP can help different people, disciplines, and institutions communicate without requiring them to share one worldview.

> **Shared grammar ≠ shared ontology.**

Its own grammar is corrigible too:

> **FSP map ≠ inquiry itself.**

### Public routes from this page

Primary:
- Explore Critical Thinking
- Explore Inquiry & Science
- Explore Domains

Secondary:
- Read the Standard explanation
- Inspect the Deep/Formal architecture

## Exclude from QUICK

Do not expose on the first screen:

- complete Twelve-Tool inventory;
- Failure Atlas codes;
- formal translation schemas;
- full provenance/standing matrices;
- grammar-change procedures;
- long Joint Task Space examples;
- technical remainder taxonomy.

The QUICK page should create orientation, not mastery.

# 3. STANDARD — “Extended Critical Thinking and Joint Task Spaces”

## Purpose

Give an interested reader a coherent explanation of what the pieces do together and what larger capability FSP is trying to create.

Target: approximately 2,500–4,000 words in final public prose.

## Required sections

### A. From traditional to Extended Critical Thinking

Retain:
- argument analysis;
- logic;
- evidence;
- induction, deduction, abduction, analogy;
- causal reasoning;
- sources;
- fallacies;
- alternatives and counterexamples.

Extend upstream:
- attention;
- map formation;
- assembly history;
- provenance;
- framing;
- perspective;
- background conditions.

Extend downstream:
- standing;
- authorization;
- institutional amplification;
- consequences;
- capability;
- repair;
- REOPEN.

Canonical sentence:

> **Extended Critical Thinking is traditional critical thinking embedded within a governed inquiry architecture.**

### B. How the devices differ

Use the functional grammar:

```text
ABCD = broad attention scaffold
Tool = selective inquiry operator
Firewall = transition-governance constraint
Failure = recurrent breakdown pattern
Claim Ceiling = bounded-conclusion rule
REOPEN = temporal corrigibility rule
Authorial Return = return to lived human authorship
Meta-REOPEN = corrigibility of the grammar itself
```

Public explanation of Firewall:

> **You may cross, but show the license.**

### C. Translation without reduction

Explain:
- domain-native ontology remains local;
- selected inquiry relations can still become mutually inspectable;
- provenance and remainder travel with translations;
- translation is lossy and must not be represented as identity.

Firewalls:

> **Translation ≠ identity.**

> **Compression ≠ semantic equivalence.**

### D. Cooperation without convergence

Explain that participants need not agree on:
- metaphysics;
- final values;
- complete explanations;
- one disciplinary method.

They may still coordinate around:
- observations;
- constraints;
- uncertainty;
- standing;
- authorization boundaries;
- reversible actions;
- monitoring;
- REOPEN conditions.

### E. Joint Task Spaces

Define:

> A Joint Task Space is a governed coordination surface in which heterogeneous participants can contribute domain-native maps, evidence, constraints, uncertainty, recommendations, permissions, and repair conditions without surrendering their local identities.

Show:

```text
domain-native inquiry
→ translation with provenance + remainder
→ shared task representation
→ Tool-guided inspection
→ Firewall-governed transitions
→ bounded joint judgment
→ standing / authorization partition
→ coordinated action
→ consequence
→ REOPEN
```

### F. Continuous intercommunication

Explain that the goal is not a final synthesis but maintained, auditable movement among partial syntheses.

### G. Recursive corrigibility

End with:

> **FSP map ≠ inquiry itself.**

and explain when grammar-level REOPEN is required.

## STANDARD claim ceiling

The Standard layer explains a capability architecture.

It does not claim that FSP has already solved interdisciplinary coordination or institutional fragmentation.

# 4. DEEP / FORMAL — “Translation Governance and Cross-Domain Interoperability”

## Purpose

Provide a reference architecture for implementation, comparison, adversarial testing, institutional application, and future executable formalization.

The Deep/Formal layer should be inspectable by researchers, designers, and advanced readers without becoming a prerequisite for using FSP.

## A. Translation object

A cross-domain translated object should be able to expose, where applicable:

```text
SOURCE_DOMAIN
SOURCE_OBJECT
SOURCE_REPRESENTATION
PROVENANCE
OBSERVATION_STATUS
INFERENCE_STATUS
ASSUMPTIONS
TRANSLATION_TARGET
TRANSLATED_REPRESENTATION
REMAINDER
CLAIM_CEILING
STANDING
RECOMMENDATION_STATUS
AUTHORIZATION_STATUS
REOPEN_CONDITIONS
RETURN_TARGET
```

Not every domain must populate every field.

Missing/not-applicable fields must remain distinguishable from unknown fields.

## B. Translation invariants

1. Provenance must not disappear through translation.
2. Translation may compress but must not silently strengthen a claim.
3. Remainder must be recordable.
4. Source-domain meaning must remain recoverable.
5. Standing must not be inferred from expertise alone.
6. Recommendation must not silently become authorization.
7. Shared field names must not imply ontological identity.
8. Model or representation success must not settle ontology.
9. Institutional amplification requires renewed claim-ceiling review.
10. Every consequential cross-domain transfer preserves REOPEN conditions.
11. Deep inspection preserves a Return path.
12. Grammar-level failures may REOPEN the schema itself.

## C. Remainder

**Remainder** is what cannot be carried cleanly into the shared representation without loss, controversy, uncertainty, or distortion.

Possible statuses:

- NONE_IDENTIFIED
- DOMAIN_NATIVE_ONLY
- PARTIALLY_TRANSLATED
- CONTESTED
- UNKNOWN
- DEFERRED

Remainder is not noise to eliminate.

It is governance information.

> **Untranslated remainder ≠ irrelevant remainder.**

## D. Standing

Standing should be represented separately from:
- knowledge;
- expertise;
- causal power;
- institutional role;
- practical ability;
- moral legitimacy.

A participant may know something without being authorized to decide.

A participant may possess institutional authority without possessing epistemic authority over every relevant claim.

## E. Authorization states

Preserve:

```text
GENERATED
POSSIBLE
RECOMMENDED
AUTHORIZED
IMPLEMENTED
REVIEWED
```

with the constitutional firewall:

> **Generate ≠ Recommend ≠ Authorize.**

## F. Cross-domain interoperability

Interoperability should be judged by whether participants can recover:

- what the other domain is claiming;
- what supports it;
- what assumptions travel;
- what does not travel;
- what remains uncertain;
- who has standing;
- what action status applies;
- what would trigger revision.

Interoperability does not require conceptual identity.

## G. Grammar-level REOPEN protocol

Trigger candidates:
- recurring translation loss;
- domain-native mismatch;
- false equivalence;
- standing error;
- excessive complexity;
- omitted actor or capability;
- failed adversarial case;
- unusable public projection;
- repeated workaround;
- framework-induced closure.

Repair sequence:

```text
detect failure
→ localize layer
→ classify failure
→ attempt smallest repair
→ preserve invariants
→ rerun domain/comparator regressions
→ either retain grammar or reopen canonical layer
```

## H. Formal claim ceiling

This layer defines a representation-governance architecture.

It does not imply that all domains can or should be fully formalized.

> **Formal translatability ≠ exhaustive representability.**
